# Regularization of Normalizing Flows, and of Energy-Based Models

This survey covers how normalizing flows are regularized, from RealNVP and Glow to the transformer flows of 2025 and 2026, and closes with energy-based models (EBMs), the other likelihood-based family whose training needs regularization. Flows are trained by exact maximum likelihood, so they have no adversarial game to stabilize; their regularization serves other goals: well-defined likelihoods on discrete or low-dimensional data, numerically stable inversion, simple learned dynamics, and samples of high quality.

A companion survey, [[2026.003__gan_regularization__1-survey]], covers GANs and gives a general taxonomy of regularization (its Part I). This survey summarizes the parts of that taxonomy that flows need in Part I and refers back for the rest. Flow matching, which replaced likelihood training for most continuous flows, is covered by [[2026.001__flow_matching__1-survey]] and is out of scope here, as are diffusion models, except where flows borrow from them.

The toolkit implements no normalizing flow today. It does implement several building blocks that invertible residual flows need, notably spectral normalization and an exact operator norm for depthwise 1D convolutions. Part VIII maps the literature to these blocks and names what is missing.

## How to read this survey

- **Summary of findings** (next section) gives the conclusions in one page.
- **Part I** gives the background: the general regularization tools that recur for flows.
- **Part II** introduces the flow families and shows where regularization enters each one.
- **Parts III to V** are the core: dequantization and noise, Lipschitz-constrained residual flows, and the stability of coupling and autoregressive flows. Each contains a short derivation.
- **Part VI** covers continuous flows and flows trained against an energy; **Part VII** covers EBMs.
- **Part VIII** is for implementers.

References use the `[Author, Year]` style of the usage guides, with links collected at the end. Where this survey states a hyperparameter value from a paper, it is the value the paper reports for its main experiments.

## Summary of findings

1. **Noise is the most important regularizer of modern flows.** Uniform dequantization made likelihoods on pixels well-defined; Gaussian noise augmentation at a larger scale, followed by score-based denoising with the flow's own likelihood, made transformer flows (TarFlow, STARFlow) competitive with diffusion models in sample quality ([Zhai et al., 2025]; [Gu et al., 2025a]). The noise level is a central hyperparameter, and recent work argues for one constant level rather than per-sample levels ([Jiang et al., 2026]).
2. **Lipschitz constraints are a precondition, not an add-on, for invertible residual flows.** A residual branch with $\mathrm{Lip}(g) \le c < 1$ makes the block invertible and bounds both directions of the map; the price is capacity, since each block can change the log-density by at most $d \log(1 + c)$.
3. **Numerical invertibility needs bounds on the inverse.** Affine coupling flows can have exploding inverses ([Behrmann et al., 2021]); bounded scale functions, additive couplings, zero-initialized outputs, and bi-Lipschitz regularization prevent them.
4. **Continuous flows need regularized dynamics.** Kinetic-energy and Jacobian penalties select simple, optimal-transport-like paths and cut training cost ([Finlay et al., 2020]).
5. **EBMs regularize like GAN discriminators.** Energy-magnitude penalties, spectral normalization, and gradient penalties stabilize contrastive-divergence training; newer objectives (energy matching, equilibrium matching) are well-posed by construction.
6. **For `dlk`,** an invertible residual 1D flow would need four additions to the existing spectral normalization: a coefficient $c < 1$, an operator-norm bound for full convolutions, a 1-Lipschitz smooth activation, and a log-determinant estimator (Part VIII).

## Terminology

- **Normalizing flow**: an invertible, differentiable map $f_\theta$ from data $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ to a latent $z = f_\theta(x)$ with a simple base density $p_Z$, usually $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Sampling uses $x = f_\theta^{-1}(z)$.
- **Log-determinant**: $\log |\det J_f(x)|$, the change in log-volume under $f$; it enters the likelihood.
- **Dequantization**: adding noise to discrete data so that a continuous density can model it.
- **Noise augmentation**: training on $x + \sigma \epsilon$ with Gaussian $\epsilon$ at a larger scale than dequantization needs, to smooth the density.
- **Bi-Lipschitz**: $f$ and $f^{-1}$ both Lipschitz. Write $L_f = \mathrm{Lip}(f)$ and $L_{f^{-1}} = \mathrm{Lip}(f^{-1})$.
- **Contraction**: a map with Lipschitz constant below 1.
- **Coupling layer**: splits $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and transforms $x_2$ by an invertible elementwise map whose parameters depend on $x_1$ only.
- **Residual flow**: a flow of blocks $F(x) = x + g(x)$ with $\mathrm{Lip}(g) < 1$.
- **CNF**: continuous normalizing flow, defined by an ODE $\dot z = v_\theta(z, t)$.
- **Hutchinson estimator**: $\mathrm{tr}(A) = \mathbb{E}_v [v^\top A v]$ for random $v$ with $\mathbb{E}[v v^\top] = I$.
- **EBM**: energy-based model, $p_\theta(x) = \exp(-E_\theta(x)) / Z_\theta$.

# Part I: Background from the General Taxonomy

The companion survey's Part I classifies regularization into parameter-norm penalties, Lipschitz and spectral control, input-space smoothness, stochastic regularization, normalization, data-side methods, and optimization-side methods. Four of these recur in flows. This part summarizes them; the companion survey has the details and the references.

## I.1 Lipschitz and spectral control

For a feed-forward network, $\mathrm{Lip}(f) \le \prod_l \mathrm{Lip}(\phi_l) \, \sigma(W_l)$, and for a residual block $x \mapsto x + g(x)$, $\mathrm{Lip} \le 1 + \mathrm{Lip}(g)$. **Spectral normalization** ([Miyato et al., 2018]) rescales each weight by its largest singular value, estimated by one power-iteration step per training forward pass; the estimate can only underestimate $\sigma$, so the normalized weight can drift slightly above 1 between steps. Flows use a scaled variant, $W \mapsto c \, W / \sigma(W)$ with a coefficient $c < 1$ (Part IV).

For a convolution, PyTorch's `spectral_norm` normalizes the kernel reshaped into an $(C_\text{out}, C_\text{in} \cdot k)$ matrix. The convolution's operator norm can exceed that matrix's norm by up to $\sqrt{k}$ ([Sedghi et al., 2019]). With circular padding, the operator norm is the largest singular value over the per-frequency matrices of the kernel's Fourier transform. Power iteration on the operator itself ([Gouk et al., 2021]; [Farnia et al., 2019]) or Gram iteration ([Delattre et al., 2023]; [Delattre et al., 2024]) gives the true norm or a certified bound. For a GAN critic the $\sqrt{k}$ gap only loosens a bound; for an invertible residual flow it can break the contraction that invertibility relies on (Section IV.5).

**Lipschitz-aware activations** matter more in flows than elsewhere. Residual flows need activations that are 1-Lipschitz and whose derivative does not saturate, because the log-determinant's gradient depends on second derivatives of the network (Section IV.2).

## I.2 Noise injection is Tikhonov regularization

[Bishop, 1995] showed that training with small additive input noise $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ is, to second order, equivalent to a penalty on the input derivatives: for a squared loss, $\mathbb{E}_\xi \|f(x + \xi) - y\|^2 \approx \|f(x) - y\|^2 + \sigma^2 \|J_f(x)\|_F^2$ near the optimum. For a density model, training on noisy data fits the smoothed density $p * \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$, which penalizes sharp densities. This identity underlies dequantization, noise augmentation (Part III), and denoising score matching (Part VII). The toolkit's feature-noise survey ([[2025.003__feature_noise__1-survey]]) covers noise inside networks.

## I.3 Normalization

Batch normalization is invertible in eval mode but couples samples in train mode, which makes its Jacobian, and hence the log-determinant, depend on the batch. RealNVP used batch normalization with running statistics ([Dinh et al., 2017]); Glow replaced it by **activation normalization**, a per-channel affine layer initialized from the statistics of a first batch ([Kingma and Dhariwal, 2018]). Normalizations inside the coupling or residual networks (not on the flow's main path) need not be invertible, but in residual flows they enter the Lipschitz bound, and scale-invariant normalizations are not Lipschitz (companion survey, Section I.6).

## I.4 Weight decay, initialization, and precision

Flows inherit the standard optimizer regularizers: TarFlow trains with AdamW, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and betas $(0.9, 0.95)$ ([Zhai et al., 2025]). Two choices specific to flows act as regularizers at the start of training: **zero-initialized outputs** of the coupling networks make every layer the identity at initialization (Glow; TarFlow initializes its scale and shift heads to zero), and **orthogonal initialization** of invertible linear layers keeps them well-conditioned. Numerical precision also matters more than for other models: the log-determinant is a sum of many terms, and TarFlow uses bfloat16 for training but float32 for likelihood evaluation, "necessary to avoid numerical issues".

# Part II: Flow Families and Where Regularization Enters

## II.1 The likelihood

A flow $f = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_1$ is trained by maximum likelihood with the change-of-variables formula,

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log p_Z\big(f(x)\big) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \left| \det J_{f_k}(x_{k-1}) \right|, \qquad x_0 = x, \quad x_k = f_k(x_{k-1}).$$

The two terms pull in opposite directions: the first rewards mapping data to high-density regions of $p_Z$ (contraction toward the origin), the second rewards expanding volume. A flow that contracts a region of data to a point gains unbounded likelihood unless the second term stops it, which is why the data's support matters (Section III.1). Maximum likelihood is a proper scoring rule and needs no adversarial game, so flows avoid the convergence problems of GANs; their failure modes are instead numerical and statistical.

## II.2 The families

| Family | Block | Inverse | Log-determinant | Representative work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coupling | $y_2 = h(x_2; \theta(x_1))$, $y_1 = x_1$ | closed form | sum of elementwise log-derivatives | NICE ([Dinh et al., 2015]), RealNVP ([Dinh et al., 2017]), Glow ([Kingma and Dhariwal, 2018]), neural spline flows ([Durkan et al., 2019]) |
| Autoregressive | $y_i = h(x_i; \theta(x_{<i}))$ | sequential | sum of elementwise log-derivatives | MAF ([Papamakarios et al., 2017]), IAF ([Kingma et al., 2016]), TarFlow ([Zhai et al., 2025]), STARFlow ([Gu et al., 2025a]) |
| Residual | $F(x) = x + g(x)$, $\mathrm{Lip}(g) < 1$ | fixed-point iteration | stochastic power series | i-ResNet ([Behrmann et al., 2019]), Residual Flows ([Chen et al., 2019]) |

# Part III: Why Flows Need Regularization, and Dequantization and Noise

## III.1 Failure modes

1. **Degenerate likelihoods on discrete or low-dimensional data.** Pixel values are discrete. A continuous density can place a spike of arbitrary height on each discrete value, so the continuous likelihood of discrete data is unbounded. Data on a low-dimensional manifold have the same problem: a flow can collapse the normal directions and gain likelihood without limit.
2. **Numerical invertibility.** Invertibility in exact arithmetic does not imply stable inversion in floating point. [Behrmann et al., 2021] showed that common architectures have exploding inverses (Part V).
3. **Topological and expressivity limits.** A bi-Lipschitz map preserves the topology of the support, so a flow from a Gaussian to a multimodal or disconnected distribution needs large Lipschitz constants ([Cornish et al., 2020]). [Verine et al., 2023] derived lower bounds on the total-variation error of bi-Lipschitz flows for such targets, and [Iske and Schönlieb, 2026] argue that the limits come from uniform Lipschitz bounds and prove full expressivity for bi-Lipschitz maps with non-uniform bounds.
4. **Likelihood does not regularize semantics.** Flows trained on CIFAR-10 assign higher likelihood to SVHN than to CIFAR-10 test images ([Nalisnick et al., 2019]). [Kirichenko et al., 2020] traced this to the inductive bias of coupling layers, which learn local pixel correlations rather than semantic content, and showed that changing the coupling masks or adding a bottleneck to the coupling networks helps out-of-distribution detection. Regularization of the likelihood alone does not fix this; architecture does.
5. **Stiff dynamics in continuous flows** (Part VI).
6. **Overfitting.** Flows have many parameters per data dimension and overfit small datasets; the usual tools (weight decay, early stopping on validation likelihood, noise) apply.

## III.2 Uniform dequantization

**Uniform dequantization** adds $u \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1)^d$ to integer pixel values and trains a continuous density $p$ on $x + u$. The discrete probability of $x$ under the model is the mass of the unit cube at $x$, $P(x) = \int_{[0,1)^d} p(x + u) \, du$, and Jensen's inequality gives

$$\log P(x) = \log \int_{[0,1)^d} p(x + u) \, du \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{u \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1)^d} \big[\log p(x + u)\big],$$

so maximizing the continuous log-likelihood of dequantized data maximizes a lower bound on the discrete log-likelihood, and the continuous likelihood can no longer diverge ([Theis et al., 2016]). The bound is loose where $p$ varies within a cube. RealNVP and Glow use uniform dequantization ([Dinh et al., 2017]; [Kingma and Dhariwal, 2018]).

## III.3 Variational and categorical dequantization

**Variational dequantization** (Flow++, [Ho et al., 2019]) learns the noise distribution $q(u \mid x)$ on the unit cube with a conditional flow and optimizes the evidence lower bound

$$\log P(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_{u \sim q(\cdot \mid x)} \left[\log p(x + u) - \log q(u \mid x)\right],$$

which is tight when $q(u \mid x) \propto p(x + u)$. The learned noise puts mass where the density is smooth, which tightens the bound and improves test likelihood. SurVAE flows ([Nielsen et al., 2020]) unify dequantization with other non-bijective layers: rounding is a surjection, and dequantization is its stochastic inverse. Argmax flows ([Hoogeboom et al., 2021]) extend dequantization to categorical data.

## III.4 Gaussian noise as a regularizer

Adding Gaussian noise with scale $\sigma$ to training data fits the smoothed density $p_\sigma = p * \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ instead of $p$. The smoothed density has full support and bounded derivatives, so it removes the degeneracy of Section III.1 for manifold data, and by Section I.2 it penalizes sharp densities. [Rothfuss et al., 2019] showed for conditional density estimation that noise on inputs and targets is a smoothness regularizer on the learned density and consistently reduces overfitting. **SoftFlow** ([Kim et al., 2020]) adds noise of a random magnitude, conditions the flow on the magnitude, and samples at a small magnitude, so the flow can model data on low-dimensional manifolds.

The price is bias: samples come from $p_\sigma$, which has its covariance inflated by $\sigma^2 I$. Older flows kept $\sigma$ small to limit the bias. Transformer flows instead chose a large $\sigma$ and removed the bias after sampling.

## III.5 Noise augmentation and denoising in transformer flows

**TarFlow** ([Zhai et al., 2025]) is a masked autoregressive flow whose autoregression runs over image patches with causal transformer blocks, alternating the direction between blocks. It showed that normalizing flows can produce samples competitive with diffusion models, with three ingredients: Gaussian noise augmentation during training, a score-based denoising step after sampling, and guidance. The authors call noise augmentation critical. Their best configurations:

| Task | Training input noise |
| --- | --- |
| Unconditional ImageNet $64^2$, likelihood | uniform $\mathcal{U}(0, 1/128)$, dequantization |
| Unconditional and conditional ImageNet $64^2$, generation | $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$ |
| Conditional ImageNet $128^2$ | $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.15^2)$ |
| Conditional AFHQ $256^2$ | $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.07^2)$ |

For generation, uniform dequantization noise failed: "sampling experiences constant numerical issues and was not able to produce sensible outputs". The sweep on conditional ImageNet $64^2$ covered $\sigma \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5\}$. Likelihood and sample quality thus want different noise levels: the small uniform noise of dequantization for the best bound, a larger Gaussian noise for samples.

**Why the flow can denoise its own samples.** A flow trained on noisy data models $p_\sigma$, and its log-density is differentiable. **Tweedie's formula** gives the posterior mean of the clean sample from the score of the noisy density. Differentiating $p_\sigma(\tilde x) = \int p(x) \, \mathcal{N}(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I) \, dx$ under the integral,

$$\nabla p_\sigma(\tilde x) = \int p(x) \, \mathcal{N}(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I) \, \frac{x - \tilde x}{\sigma^2} \, dx \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde x] = \tilde x + \sigma^2 \, \nabla_{\tilde x} \log p_\sigma(\tilde x).$$

TarFlow samples $\tilde x = f^{-1}(z)$ and returns $\hat x = \tilde x + \sigma^2 \nabla \log p_\theta(\tilde x)$, using the flow's own score, at the cost of one forward and one backward pass. The denoiser comes for free because the flow is an exact density, which a GAN or a VAE is not.

**Guidance.** For conditional models, TarFlow drops the class label with probability 0.1 during training and mixes the conditional and unconditional predictions of each autoregressive step, $\tilde\mu = (1 + w) \mu(\cdot; c) - w \mu(\cdot; \emptyset)$ and likewise for the scale. For unconditional models, it contrasts the model with a version whose attention logits are divided by a temperature. Label dropout is itself a regularizer: the same network must model the conditional and the marginal density.

**Successors.**

- **STARFlow** ([Gu et al., 2025a]) scales TarFlow to high resolution and text conditioning with a deep-shallow design (one deep transformer block holds most of the capacity), modeling in the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder, and a new guidance algorithm. **STARFlow-V** ([Gu et al., 2025b]) extends it to video, with noise augmentation and a learned causal denoiser trained by flow-score matching.
- **iTARFlow** ([Chen et al., 2026]) trains the autoregressive flow across a range of noise levels with an end-to-end likelihood objective and denoises iteratively at sampling time, a step toward diffusion-style sampling with a flow.
- **JetFormer** ([Tschannen et al., 2025]) trains a normalizing flow jointly with an autoregressive transformer on raw images and uses a **noise curriculum**: Gaussian noise added to the images is reduced over training, which makes the model learn high-level content first. Its flow, Jet ([Kolesnikov et al., 2024]), is a coupling flow of plain vision transformer blocks without multiscale structure.
- **Constant versus per-sample noise.** [Jiang et al., 2026] argue that per-sample noise levels, borrowed from autoencoder training, fit flows poorly: a flow learns one unconditional bijection, so per-sample noise forces it to model a mixture of differently perturbed distributions with one fixed map. They use one constant level for all samples ($\sigma = 0.4$ in their latent space).
- **SimFlow** ([Zhao et al., 2025]) trains a latent flow end to end with its autoencoder and fixes the encoder's variance to a constant (for example 0.5) instead of predicting it. The constant variance plays the role of noise augmentation, without separate noising and denoising steps, and makes joint training stable. SimFlow reports an ImageNet-256 FID of 2.15, against 2.40 for STARFlow.
- **FARMER** ([Zheng et al., 2025]) combines an autoregressive flow with an autoregressive model on pixels and splits the flow's latent channels into informative and redundant groups, a learned dimension reduction that keeps the autoregressive model from spending capacity on noise.

## III.6 Synthesis: what noise does for a flow

| Role | Noise | Effect | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Make the likelihood well-defined | uniform, small | bound on the discrete likelihood | RealNVP, Glow |
| Tighten the bound | learned, on the unit cube | better test likelihood | Flow++ |
| Smooth the density | Gaussian, moderate to large | stable sampling, fewer artifacts | TarFlow, STARFlow |
| Remove the bias | none (post-hoc) | Tweedie denoising with the flow's own score | TarFlow |
| Order the learning | Gaussian, annealed | coarse content first | JetFormer |
| Avoid a mixture of noise levels | Gaussian, constant | one bijection for one smoothed density | [Jiang et al., 2026] |

The common thread with GANs (companion survey, Section III.3) and EBMs (Part VII) is Bishop's identity: noise is a smoothness penalty that costs no second derivatives.

# Part IV: Lipschitz-Constrained Residual Flows

## IV.1 Invertible residual networks

**i-ResNets** ([Behrmann et al., 2019]) make a residual block $F(x) = x + g(x)$ invertible by constraining $\mathrm{Lip}(g) \le c < 1$. Three consequences follow, each with a short proof.

**Invertibility.** For a given $y$, the inverse $x = F^{-1}(y)$ is the fixed point of $T(x) = y - g(x)$, which is a contraction with constant $c$. By the Banach fixed-point theorem the iteration $x_{k+1} = y - g(x_k)$ converges to the unique fixed point, with error

$$\|x_n - x^*\| \le \frac{c^n}{1 - c} \, \|x_1 - x_0\|.$$

At $c = 0.9$, reducing the error by $10^{-6}$ takes about 130 iterations, so $c$ trades inversion cost against capacity.

**Bi-Lipschitz bounds.** The forward bound is $\mathrm{Lip}(F) \le 1 + c$. For the inverse, $\|x - x'\| \le \|F(x) - F(x')\| + \|g(x) - g(x')\| \le \|F(x) - F(x')\| + c \|x - x'\|$, so

$$\mathrm{Lip}(F^{-1}) \le \frac{1}{1 - c}.$$

A stack of $K$ blocks has $\mathrm{Lip} \le (1 + c)^K$ and $\mathrm{Lip}(F^{-1}) \le (1 - c)^{-K}$. Both directions are bounded, which rules out the exploding inverses of Part V.

**Log-determinant.** All eigenvalues of $J_g$ have modulus at most $c$, so the power series of the matrix logarithm converges:

$$\log \det(I + J_g) = \mathrm{tr} \log(I + J_g) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k} \, \mathrm{tr}\big(J_g^k\big).$$

Each trace is estimated with the Hutchinson estimator, $\mathrm{tr}(J_g^k) \approx v^\top J_g^k v$, computed by $k$ vector–Jacobian products. i-ResNets truncate the series after $n$ terms, which biases the estimate by at most $-d \big(\log(1 - c) + \sum_{k=1}^{n} c^k / k\big)$.

**A capacity bound.** The eigenvalues of $I + J_g$ lie in a disk of radius $c$ around 1, so

$$d \log(1 - c) \le \log |\det J_F(x)| \le d \log(1 + c).$$

Each block can change the log-density by at most $d \log(1 + c)$. A target density that is much more concentrated than the base distribution needs many blocks. This is the capacity cost of the Lipschitz constraint, and it is the main argument for implicit flows (Section IV.3).

**Enforcing the constraint.** i-ResNets apply spectral normalization with coefficient $c = 0.9$ to every layer of $g$, $W \mapsto c \, W / \sigma(W)$ when $\sigma(W) > c$, and use 1-Lipschitz activations, so that $\mathrm{Lip}(g) \le c$ by the product bound. For convolutions they estimate the operator norm by power iteration on the convolution itself, following [Gouk et al., 2021], not on the reshaped kernel. Here the Lipschitz constraint is not a regularizer in the classic sense; it is the condition for the model to be defined. It also acts as one: it bounds both directions of the map and limits how sharp the learned density can be.

## IV.2 Residual Flows

**Residual Flows** ([Chen et al., 2019]) removed the bias of the truncated series and the memory cost of backpropagating through it.

**Unbiased log-determinant.** A "Russian roulette" estimator draws a random truncation $n \sim p(N)$ and reweights each term by the probability that it is included:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \Delta_k = \mathbb{E}_{n \sim p(N)} \left[\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta_k}{P(N \ge k)}\right].$$

With $\Delta_k = (-1)^{k+1} v^\top J_g^k v / k$, this is an unbiased estimate of the log-determinant, so the training objective is an unbiased estimate of the log-likelihood.

**Memory-efficient gradient.** Differentiating the series term by term would store $n$ vector–Jacobian products. Residual Flows instead use a Neumann series for the gradient,

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log \det\big(I + J_g(x, \theta)\big) = \mathbb{E}_{n, v} \left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{P(N \ge k)} \, v^\top J_g^k\right) \frac{\partial J_g(x, \theta)}{\partial \theta} \, v\right],$$

which needs only one backward pass through $J_g$ after the vector $\sum_k (\cdot) v^\top J_g^k$ is accumulated without storing the graph.

**LipSwish.** The gradient of the log-determinant involves second derivatives of $g$. Activations whose derivative saturates at 1 (ELU, softplus) have a second derivative that vanishes exactly where the activation is steepest, which slows learning when the Lipschitz constraint is tight. Residual Flows introduced

$$\mathrm{LipSwish}(z) = \frac{z \, \mathrm{sigmoid}(\beta z)}{1.1},$$

with a learnable $\beta > 0$; Swish has Lipschitz constant about 1.1 for every $\beta$, so dividing by 1.1 makes LipSwish 1-Lipschitz, and its derivative does not saturate.

**Other changes.** Residual Flows raised the spectral normalization coefficient to $c = 0.98$, from the 0.9 of i-ResNets, and generalized the Lipschitz constraint to induced mixed norms, $\|W\|_{p \to q}$, which can give tighter per-layer bounds than the spectral norm.

## IV.3 Extensions and the capacity trade-off

- **Invertible DenseNets** ([Perugachi-Diaz et al., 2021]) extend the construction to dense blocks, with a learned weighting of the concatenated features that keeps the block a contraction, and a concatenated LipSwish activation.
- **Implicit normalizing flows** ([Lu et al., 2021]) define the map implicitly, through $F(x, z) = 0$ with two residual maps, which relaxes the per-block capacity bound of Section IV.1: an implicit block can represent maps that a residual block with $\mathrm{Lip}(g) < 1$ cannot.
- **Expressivity.** The capacity cost of uniform Lipschitz bounds is the central trade-off of this family. [Verine et al., 2023] quantify what bi-Lipschitz flows cannot approximate, and [Iske and Schönlieb, 2026] show that bi-Lipschitz maps with non-uniform bounds are fully expressive, which suggests constraints that vary over the input space.

## IV.4 Practical checklist for a residual flow

| Component | Requirement | Standard choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear and convolution layers | $\sigma \le c$ for the true operator norm | SN with coefficient $c \in [0.9, 0.98]$; operator power iteration for convolutions |
| Activations | 1-Lipschitz, non-saturating derivative | LipSwish |
| Normalization in $g$ | Lipschitz-bounded | none, or a bounded affine layer |
| Residual sum | exactly $x + g(x)$ | no scaling on the identity path |
| Log-determinant | unbiased, low memory | Russian roulette with Neumann gradient |
| Inversion | converges geometrically at rate $c$ | fixed-point iteration, a few tens of steps |

## IV.5 The convolution operator norm in residual flows

For a GAN critic, PyTorch's reshaped-matrix spectral normalization only loosens a Lipschitz bound by up to $\sqrt{k}$. For a residual flow it can break the model: if the true operator norm of a convolution in $g$ exceeds $1 / \prod(\text{other bounds})$, then $\mathrm{Lip}(g)$ can exceed 1, the fixed-point iteration need not converge, the power series of the log-determinant need not converge, and the model may not be invertible. The one-channel averaging kernel $(1/k, \dots, 1/k)$ shows the gap: its reshaped matrix has norm $1/\sqrt{k}$, so reshaped SN would scale it up by $\sqrt{k}$, to an operator with norm $\sqrt{k}$. Residual flows therefore need the operator norm itself: by power iteration on the convolution and its transpose, by the Fourier view for circular padding ([Sedghi et al., 2019]), or by a certified upper bound such as Gram iteration ([Delattre et al., 2024]). For depthwise convolutions, the Fourier view reduces to the largest DFT magnitude over all channels, which is what the toolkit's `DepthwiseSpectralNorm` computes (Part VIII).

# Part V: Stabilizing Coupling and Autoregressive Flows

## V.1 Exploding inverses: a worked analysis

Coupling flows are invertible by construction, so they need no Lipschitz constraint to be defined, but they can be numerically non-invertible. [Behrmann et al., 2021] showed that common architectures suffer from **exploding inverses**: the change-of-variables formula fails on in- and out-of-distribution data, memory-saving backpropagation (which recomputes activations by inversion) computes wrong gradients, and flows cannot sample. They traced the problem to the bi-Lipschitz constants of the building blocks. Two blocks show why.

**Additive coupling** (NICE). $y_1 = x_1$, $y_2 = x_2 + t(x_1)$, with inverse $x_2 = y_2 - t(y_1)$. The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ J_t & I \end{pmatrix}$, so

$$\mathrm{Lip}(F) \le 1 + \mathrm{Lip}(t), \qquad \mathrm{Lip}(F^{-1}) \le 1 + \mathrm{Lip}(t),$$

and the block is volume preserving. Both directions are bounded as soon as $t$ is Lipschitz.

**Affine coupling** (RealNVP, Glow). $y_2 = x_2 \odot \exp(s(x_1)) + t(x_1)$, with inverse $x_2 = (y_2 - t(y_1)) \odot \exp(-s(y_1))$. The Jacobian blocks are

$$\frac{\partial y_2}{\partial x_2} = \mathrm{diag}\big(e^{s(x_1)}\big), \qquad \frac{\partial y_2}{\partial x_1} = \mathrm{diag}\big(x_2 \odot e^{s(x_1)}\big) J_s + J_t.$$

The forward map's Lipschitz constant grows with $|x_2|$, through the off-diagonal block, so it has no global bound. The inverse has $\partial x_2 / \partial y_2 = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-s})$, which explodes when $s$ is very negative, and an off-diagonal block that grows with $|y_2 - t|$. Inputs far from the training data, where $s$ and $t$ were never constrained, can produce arbitrarily large inverse sensitivities.

## V.2 Bounding the scale

The usual remedy bounds the scale function, which bounds both diagonal blocks:

| Parametrization | Scale factor $e^{s}$ | Range | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| unbounded | $\exp(h)$ | $(0, \infty)$ | early RealNVP variants |
| scaled tanh | $\exp(\alpha \tanh(h / \alpha))$ | $[e^{-\alpha}, e^{\alpha}]$ | RealNVP (learned scale), many implementations ("soft clamping") |
| shifted sigmoid | $\mathrm{sigmoid}(h + 2)$ | $(0, 1)$, near 0.88 at $h = 0$ | the Glow implementation |
| additive | 1 | $\{1\}$ | NICE, Glow ablations |

A bounded scale bounds the diagonal blocks, but not the off-diagonal blocks, which still grow with the input magnitude; on bounded data domains (images in $[-1, 1]$) this is enough in practice.

**Spline couplings** ([Durkan et al., 2019]) replace the affine map by a monotone rational-quadratic spline on an interval $[-B, B]$, with identity (linear tails) outside. The spline's derivatives are bounded below and above by construction, which bounds the elementwise Lipschitz constants in both directions, and the identity tails keep out-of-range inputs from being distorted.

## V.3 Other stabilizers

- **Zero initialization.** Glow initializes the last convolution of each coupling network to zero, so every coupling layer is the identity at initialization; TarFlow initializes its scale and shift heads to zero. Training starts from a well-conditioned map, a regularizer of the optimization path rather than of the solution.
- **Activation normalization** (Glow): a per-channel affine layer with data-dependent initialization, batch-independent and with a trivial log-determinant.
- **Invertible $1 \times 1$ convolutions** in LU form (Glow), $W = P L (U + \mathrm{diag}(s))$, whose log-determinant is $\sum_i \log |s_i|$ and whose conditioning can be monitored through $s$.
- **Residual connections in autoregressive flows.** TarFlow uses residual connections inside its transformer and across the latents of consecutive flow blocks, and ties them to training stability.
- **Bi-Lipschitz regularization.** For local invertibility (memory-saving backpropagation), [Behrmann et al., 2021] propose a flexible and efficient regularizer; for global invertibility (flows on out-of-distribution data) they recommend stable building blocks, such as additive couplings or Lipschitz-constrained residual blocks.
- **Precision.** Likelihood evaluation in float32 (TarFlow), and monitoring of the inversion error $\|f^{-1}(f(x)) - x\|$ on held-out and out-of-distribution data, are cheap diagnostics.

## V.4 Soft invertibility and alternative objectives

- **Free-form flows** ([Draxler et al., 2024]) train an arbitrary encoder $f$ and decoder $g$ as a flow, with a gradient estimator for the log-determinant and a reconstruction penalty $\beta \, \mathbb{E} \|g(f(x)) - x\|^2$ that keeps the decoder an approximate inverse. Invertibility becomes a soft constraint enforced by the loss, which frees the architecture from coupling or Lipschitz structure.
- **Regression training** (RegFlow, [Rehman et al., 2025]) replaces maximum likelihood by an $\ell_2$ regression of the flow's outputs onto targets from optimal-transport couplings or a pretrained continuous flow, plus a forward–backward self-consistency loss for numerical stability. The authors report that it trains architectures that are hard to train by maximum likelihood and is more stable, for Boltzmann generators (Section VI.3).

# Part VI: Continuous Flows and Flows Trained Against an Energy

## VI.1 Continuous normalizing flows

CNFs ([Chen et al., 2018]; FFJORD, [Grathwohl et al., 2019]) define $f$ through an ODE $\dot z_t = v_\theta(z_t, t)$ and compute the log-density by the instantaneous change of variables,

$$\frac{d \log p(z_t)}{dt} = -\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial z}(z_t, t)\right),$$

with the trace estimated by the Hutchinson estimator and one vector–Jacobian product per function evaluation. Any smooth $v_\theta$ gives an invertible map, so CNFs need no architectural constraint. Unregularized, however, they learn dynamics that need hundreds of adaptive solver steps, and the trace estimator adds variance.

## VI.2 Regularizing the dynamics

- **RNODE** ([Finlay et al., 2020]) adds a kinetic-energy term and a Jacobian Frobenius term,

  $$\lambda_K \int_0^T \|v_\theta(z_t, t)\|^2 \, dt + \lambda_J \int_0^T \|\nabla_z v_\theta(z_t, t)\|_F^2 \, dt.$$

  The kinetic term is the Benamou–Brenier objective of optimal transport: among all flows that transport $p_Z$ to the data, the one that minimizes $\int \|v\|^2$ moves along straight lines at constant speed, which an adaptive solver integrates in few steps. The Jacobian term keeps the flow smooth, and it is almost free: the Hutchinson vector–Jacobian product $\epsilon^\top \nabla_z v$ computed for the trace also gives the unbiased estimate $\|\nabla_z v\|_F^2 \approx \|\epsilon^\top \nabla_z v\|^2$. RNODE trains to the same likelihood as FFJORD with far fewer solver steps and much less training time.
- **Higher-order derivative penalties** ([Kelly et al., 2020]) penalize the $K$-th time derivative of the trajectory, computed with Taylor-mode automatic differentiation, which directly targets the local error of the solver.
- **OT-Flow** ([Onken et al., 2021]) parametrizes the velocity as the gradient of a potential, $v = -\nabla \Phi$, computes the trace exactly from the potential's Hessian, and adds the transport cost and a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman penalty from optimal-control theory.
- **STEER** ([Ghosh et al., 2020]) samples the end time of the integration randomly during training, which regularizes the dynamics at no extra cost.

Flow matching replaced likelihood training for most continuous flows after 2022: it regresses the velocity on a prescribed probability path and so builds the straight-path regularization of RNODE into the objective. The toolkit's flow matching survey ([[2026.001__flow_matching__1-survey]]) covers it.

## VI.3 Flows trained against an energy: Boltzmann generators

In scientific applications, the target is often given by an unnormalized energy $u(x)$ rather than by samples. **Boltzmann generators** ([Noé et al., 2019]) train a flow by the reverse KL divergence,

$$\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta \,\|\, p) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_Z} \left[u\big(f_\theta^{-1}(z)\big) - \log \left|\det J_{f_\theta^{-1}}(z)\right|\right] + \text{const},$$

which needs no data but is mode-seeking: the flow can collapse onto one metastable state. The standard regularizer combines the reverse KL with maximum likelihood on whatever samples are available, which covers the modes. RegFlow ([Rehman et al., 2025]) replaces maximum likelihood by regression for stability (Section V.4). The energy term plays the role that the discriminator plays in a GAN, and the entropy term $-\log|\det J|$ is what keeps the flow from collapsing; regularizing the flow's Lipschitz constant limits how sharply it can concentrate on one mode.

# Part VII: Regularization of Energy-Based Models

## VII.1 Training EBMs and where it goes wrong

An EBM defines $p_\theta(x) = \exp(-E_\theta(x)) / Z_\theta$. The gradient of the negative log-likelihood is a difference of two expectations,

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_r} [\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta} [\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)],$$

and the second needs samples from the model, usually by Langevin dynamics $x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{\eta}{2} \nabla_x E_\theta(x_k) + \sqrt{\eta} \, \xi_k$. This is contrastive divergence (CD, [Hinton, 2002]) when chains are short. EBMs and flows are both likelihood-based, but where a flow pays for the normalizing constant with an invertible architecture, an EBM pays with sampling. The training resembles a GAN in which the generator is an MCMC sampler and the discriminator is the energy: it lowers the energy of data and raises the energy of samples (companion survey, Section III.6). The failure modes are similar too:

1. **Energy divergence.** Nothing in the CD objective bounds the energy values; the difference of energies on data and samples can grow without bound.
2. **Non-smooth energy landscapes.** Sharp energies make Langevin dynamics unstable and slow to mix. For a step size $\eta$, Langevin dynamics is stable only if $\eta$ is small relative to the inverse of the energy's gradient Lipschitz constant, so smoothness of $E$ is a condition for sampling, as the Lipschitz bound is a condition for inversion in residual flows.
3. **Biased gradients.** Short-run chains do not reach the model distribution, so the learned energy is not the maximum-likelihood energy ([Nijkamp et al., 2019]; [Nijkamp et al., 2020]).

## VII.2 Explicit regularizers for CD-trained EBMs

[Du and Mordatch, 2019] scaled MCMC-trained EBMs to images with three ingredients: an L2 penalty on the energy magnitudes $\alpha \, \mathbb{E}[E_\theta(x^+)^2 + E_\theta(x^-)^2]$ on data $x^+$ and samples $x^-$, spectral normalization of the energy network to keep the landscape smooth for Langevin dynamics, and a replay buffer that initializes chains from past samples. The energy penalty is the EBM analog of ProGAN's drift penalty on critic outputs, and SN plays the same role as in SNGAN.

[Du et al., 2021] identified a gradient term of the CD objective that is commonly ignored, through the dependence of the MCMC samples on $\theta$, and showed that including it as a KL loss improves stability. They also added data augmentation and multi-scale processing. [Nijkamp et al., 2020] analyzed MCMC-based maximum likelihood and distinguished convergent learning (the energy defines a valid density) from non-convergent learning (short-run MCMC with a fixed number of steps acts as a generator), with different requirements on the noise and step size.

Joint energy-based models (JEM, [Grathwohl et al., 2020a]) reinterpret a classifier's logits as an energy and train classification and generation together. JEM training is notoriously unstable, and its successors add regularization: JEM++ ([Yang and Ji, 2021]) uses a proximal SGLD and an informative initialization to improve stability and speed. VERA ([Grathwohl et al., 2021]) replaces MCMC by a learned generator with an entropy regularizer. A 2025 study of adversarially trained EBMs ([Yin et al., 2025]) reports that the original adversarial EBM framework needed explicit $R_1$ penalties for stability, which its own adversarial training makes unnecessary, an example of the GAN toolbox transferring to EBMs.

**Stein discrepancies.** [Grathwohl et al., 2020b] train EBMs without sampling by minimizing a learned Stein discrepancy, whose critic must be constrained. They use an L2 penalty on the critic's output instead of a Lipschitz constraint, which connects to the integral probability metrics of Wasserstein GANs.

| Regularizer | Target | Analog in GANs | Analog in flows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Energy-magnitude penalty | unbounded energy differences | drift penalty | none (normalized by construction) |
| Spectral normalization of $E$ | sharp landscapes, Langevin stability | SN of the discriminator | SN with coefficient $c < 1$ in residual flows |
| Gradient penalty ($R_1$) on $E$ | sharp landscapes | $R_1$ | Jacobian penalty in CNFs |
| Replay buffer, persistent chains | short-run bias | none | none |
| Data augmentation | overfitting, mode coverage | DiffAugment, ADA | noise augmentation |
| Denoising score matching | partition function, smoothness | instance noise | Gaussian dequantization |

## VII.3 Score matching and noise as regularization

Score matching ([Hyvärinen, 2005]) avoids the partition function by matching the model's score $s_\theta = -\nabla_x E_\theta$ to the data score, through the objective $\mathbb{E}_{P_r}[\mathrm{tr}(\nabla_x s_\theta(x)) + \frac{1}{2} \|s_\theta(x)\|^2]$. Its trace term needs second derivatives; sliced score matching ([Song et al., 2020]) estimates it with random projections. **Denoising score matching** ([Vincent, 2011]) replaces it by a regression on the score of a noise kernel,

$$\mathbb{E}_{x, \tilde x} \left\| s_\theta(\tilde x) - \frac{x - \tilde x}{\sigma^2} \right\|^2, \qquad \tilde x = x + \sigma \xi,$$

which learns the score of the smoothed density $p_\sigma$. Its minimizer is the Tweedie score of Section III.5, $s^*(\tilde x) = (\mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde x] - \tilde x) / \sigma^2$, the same quantity TarFlow uses for denoising. The noise is a regularizer: it makes the target density smooth and its support full-dimensional, as instance noise does for GANs and dequantization does for flows. **Diffusion recovery likelihood** ([Gao et al., 2021]) trains a sequence of EBMs on increasingly noisy data, each by a conditional likelihood that is easy to sample with short MCMC chains. Score-based diffusion models grew from this line of work and are out of scope.

## VII.4 Recent directions (2023–2026)

- **Energy discrepancy** is a contrastive loss that needs no MCMC; [Schröder et al., 2023] extend it to discrete EBMs. **Diffusion contrastive divergence** ([Luo et al., 2023]) replaces Langevin chains by diffusion processes with no MCMC bias.
- **Energy matching** ([Balcerak et al., 2025]) learns a single time-independent scalar potential whose gradient transports noise to data along optimal-transport paths far from the data and turns into a Boltzmann distribution near it; it reports a CIFAR-10 FID of 3.97, against 8.61 for the best previous EBM. **Equilibrium matching** ([Wang and Du, 2025]) drops time conditioning entirely and learns the equilibrium gradient of an implicit energy, reporting an ImageNet-256 FID of 1.90. In both, the regularity of the learned energy comes from the matching objective rather than from penalties.
- **Equilibrium training with parallel trajectory tempering** ([Béreux et al., 2026]) reports that persistent CD chains get stuck near training examples and degrade likelihood estimates, and uses tempering to keep chains mixing.

These methods suggest that EBMs are moving from explicit regularizers (energy penalties, SN) toward objectives that are well-posed by construction, as flows moved from small dequantization noise toward noise augmentation built into the training and sampling procedure.

# Part VIII: Cross-Cutting Themes and Mapping to the Deep Learning Toolkit

## VIII.1 Themes shared by flows and EBMs

- **Smoothness is a precondition for the model's own machinery.** Inversion by fixed-point iteration needs $\mathrm{Lip}(g) < 1$, the log-determinant series needs the same, Langevin sampling needs a smooth energy, and adaptive ODE solvers need non-stiff dynamics. In GANs, smoothness serves training dynamics; here it serves inference.
- **Noise is the cheapest smoothness regularizer.** Dequantization, noise augmentation, and denoising score matching all fit a smoothed density, and Tweedie's formula turns the smoothed model's score into a denoiser.
- **Bounds in both directions.** Flows need bounds on the inverse as well as the forward map; additive couplings, bounded scales, and residual contractions provide them.
- **The capacity cost of uniform bounds.** Lipschitz-constrained flows trade expressivity for stability, and recent theory points to non-uniform bounds as the way out.

## VIII.2 What `dlk` provides toward an invertible residual flow

The toolkit implements no normalizing flow. These building blocks exist and would carry over:

| Need (Part IV) | `dlk` component | Status for a residual flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spectral normalization of linear layers | `set_spectral_norm` in `dlk/nets/spectral_norm.py`; `enable_spectral_norm` in `MLPResNet`, `ConvResNet`, `UniversalMultiLevelBlock` | bound 1, no coefficient $c < 1$ |
| Operator norm of depthwise convolutions | `DepthwiseSpectralNorm`, `set_depthwise_spectral_norm` | exact with circular padding at the DFT length; at most $1 / (1 - (k - 1)\pi / 64)$ otherwise; no coefficient |
| Operator norm of full convolutions | none | reshaped-matrix SN only, up to $\sqrt{k}$ too large |
| 1-Lipschitz smooth activation | none (`ReLU` is 1-Lipschitz but has zero second derivative; `SiLU` is about 1.1-Lipschitz) | LipSwish missing |
| Normalization-free residual blocks | `UniversalMultiLevelBlock` with `normalization=False`; EfficientNet styles with `CONV_NORM_NONE` | available |
| Residual sum without scaling | `skip_scale=1.0` | available; note that `skip_scale` scales the whole sum, not the branch |
| Orthogonal initialization | `set_init_parameters` in `dlk/nets/utils.py` | available |
| Log-determinant estimator, fixed-point inverse | none | missing |
| Dequantization and noise augmentation utilities | none in `dlk/data` | missing |

## VIII.3 Gaps, ranked

The ranking assumes the toolkit would add a 1D invertible residual flow for time series; whether to add flows at all is a separate decision.

1. **A spectral normalization coefficient.** `set_spectral_norm(layer, coefficient=c)` and the same for `DepthwiseSpectralNorm`, normalizing to $c$ when $\sigma > c$ (i-ResNet's form). Small, and also useful for GAN critics with a Lipschitz target other than 1.
2. **An operator-norm bound for full convolutions.** Power iteration on `conv1d` and `conv_transpose1d` ([Farnia et al., 2019]) or Gram iteration ([Delattre et al., 2024]). Without it, a residual flow with $k > 1$ convolutions has no guaranteed contraction (Section IV.5).
3. **LipSwish.** One line; it also tightens GAN critic bounds, where SiLU contributes a factor 1.0998 per activation.
4. **Log-determinant estimation and inversion.** Hutchinson power series with Russian roulette and the Neumann gradient ([Chen et al., 2019]), and a fixed-point inverse with a tolerance. This is the core of a residual flow and the largest piece of work.
5. **Noise utilities.** Uniform dequantization for quantized data, Gaussian noise augmentation with a constant level, and Tweedie denoising with the model's score. Noise augmentation also applies to the toolkit's GAN critics as instance noise (companion survey, gap 7).
6. **Diagnostics.** Inversion error on held-out and perturbed data, and the per-block log-determinant range against $d \log(1 \pm c)$.

## VIII.4 Open problems

- **Choosing the noise level.** TarFlow tunes $\sigma$ per resolution, JetFormer anneals it, and [Jiang et al., 2026] argue for a constant level. A principled rule (relative to the data's scale and dimension, or to the intended denoiser) is missing.
- **Exact Lipschitz control for convolutional flows on non-circular padding.** Certified bounds exist (Gram iteration), but residual flows mostly use power-iteration estimates that can underestimate.
- **Non-uniform Lipschitz constraints.** Theory says they recover expressivity; no practical parametrization has been shown at scale.
- **EBM training without MCMC bias.** Matching objectives sidestep the problem; whether they recover calibrated likelihoods is open.
