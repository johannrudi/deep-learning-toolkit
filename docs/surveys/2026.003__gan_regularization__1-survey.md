# Regularization of Neural Networks, with an In-Depth Survey for GANs

This survey covers how neural networks are regularized, from the classic papers to work published in 2026. Part I is a compact taxonomy of regularization for discriminative networks, with an emphasis on convolutional networks and the EfficientNet family. Part II goes in depth for generative adversarial networks (GANs), where the discriminator is the network that is regularized. Part III draws out the themes that cut across methods. Part IV maps the literature to the Deep Learning Toolkit (`dlk`), lists the gaps, ranks candidates for implementation, and gives a configuration guide for `dlk` critics.

A companion survey, [[2026.004__flow_regularization__1-survey]], covers normalizing flows and energy-based models. The two share the Lipschitz and noise tools of Part I; that survey refers back here for them.

The toolkit's recent work motivates the selection. Plans 2026.007 to 2026.010 added spectral normalization to `ConvResNet` and the 1D EfficientNets, five gradient and finite-difference penalties and a spectral norm penalty for Wasserstein GAN critics, an exact spectral normalization for depthwise convolutions, and network styles that let spectral normalization bind in an EfficientNet critic. The code in `dlk/` is the authoritative source for what exists; the plans in `docs/features/` are historical.

Diffusion models, flow matching, and variational autoencoders are out of scope, except where a GAN method comes from them (adversarial distillation of diffusion models, which trains GAN discriminators).

## How to read this survey

- **Summary of findings** (next section) gives the conclusions in one page.
- **Part I** is a map. Each section names the idea, gives the defining equation, and cites the key papers. Read it to place a method.
- **Part II** is the core. Section II.2 works through the smallest example that shows why GAN training needs regularization; Section II.13 is a catalogue of all discriminator penalties in one table.
- **Part IV** is for implementers. It names files and functions in `dlk` and contains code sketches.

References use the `[Author, Year]` style of the usage guides, with links collected at the end. Where this survey states a hyperparameter value from a paper, it is the value the paper reports for its main experiments; papers often tune it per dataset.

## Summary of findings

1. **The field has converged on zero-centered gradient penalties at data and samples.** $R_1$ ([Mescheder et al., 2018]) became the standard in image GANs; R3GAN ([Huang et al., 2024]) showed that $R_1 + R_2$ with a relativistic loss suffices for a GAN with almost no other tricks; GAT ([Hyun et al., 2025]) scales the same recipe to transformers.
2. **Double backward is the bottleneck, and finite differences are the answer.** Large transformer discriminators cannot use exact gradient penalties under sharded training and fused kernels. APT ([Lin et al., 2025]) and GAT replace them by finite differences on Gaussian-perturbed data, the approximated $R_1$ and $R_2$. The toolkit's finite-difference penalties were built for the same reason (`torch.compile`).
3. **Spectral normalization remains the cheap global bound,** but its product bound is loose, it shrinks signals with depth, and normalizations that are scale invariant cancel it. Modern discriminators use no normalization layers.
4. **Noise and gradient penalties are two forms of one regularizer.** Instance noise, Roth's regularizer, consistency regularization, and the approximated $R_1$ all expand to a squared input-gradient penalty ([Bishop, 1995]).
5. **Discriminator overfitting is the main problem with limited data.** Non-leaking augmentation (DiffAugment, ADA) and output-based regularizers (LeCam) address it, with strengths adapted during training.
6. **1D discriminators in practice** (audio GANs) rely on multi-scale and multi-period structure, weight normalization, and feature matching, not on gradient penalties.
7. **For `dlk`,** the largest gap is that all its gradient penalties evaluate at interpolates (WGAN-GP's choice). Zero-centered penalties at data and samples, in exact and in forward-only form, would close it at low cost (Part IV).

## Terminology

- **Regularization**: any modification of a learning algorithm that is intended to reduce its generalization error but not its training error ([Goodfellow et al., 2016], Chapter 7). For GANs, this survey also counts modifications that make training stable or convergent, because the two goals overlap in practice.
- **Explicit regularizer**: a term added to the loss, such as $\frac{\lambda}{2} \|\theta\|^2$.
- **Implicit regularization**: a bias toward certain solutions that comes from the optimizer, the architecture, or the data pipeline, with no term in the loss.
- **Lipschitz constant** of $f$: the smallest $L$ with $\|f(x) - f(x')\| \le L \|x - x'\|$ for all $x, x'$. Written $\mathrm{Lip}(f)$ or $\|f\|_L$.
- **Spectral norm** $\sigma(W)$: the largest singular value of a matrix $W$, equal to its operator norm $\|W\|_2$.
- **Spectral normalization (SN)**: the reparametrization $W \mapsto W / \sigma(W)$ ([Miyato et al., 2018a]). **Spectral norm regularization (SNR)**: the loss term $\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_l \sigma(W_l)^2$ ([Yoshida and Miyato, 2017]).
- **Critic** and **discriminator**: the second network of a GAN. "Critic" is used for Wasserstein GANs, whose second network outputs an unbounded score, and "discriminator" otherwise. The survey uses $D$ for both, $G$ for the generator, $P_r$ for the data distribution, and $P_g$ for the generated distribution.
- **Gradient penalty (GP)**: a loss term on the norm of $\nabla_x D(x)$ at chosen points. **Zero-centered** penalties push the norm toward 0 ($R_1$, $R_2$), **one-centered** penalties toward 1 (WGAN-GP), and **one-sided** penalties act only above a target (WGAN-LP).
- **Approximated $R_1$ (aR1)**: a forward-only estimate of $R_1$ by finite differences under Gaussian perturbations.
- **Scale-invariant layer**: a layer $N$ with $N(c z) = N(z)$ for every $c > 0$, such as batch, layer, or group normalization without bias in front of it.
- **Mode collapse**: a generator that covers only part of the data distribution.
- **Leaking**: augmentations of the discriminator's inputs that the generator learns to reproduce.
- **F**: the cost of one critic forward pass, the unit of the toolkit's cost estimates.

# Part I: A Taxonomy of Regularization

## I.1 What regularization does, and how to classify it

Classic learning theory treats regularization as capacity control: a penalty or a constraint shrinks the hypothesis class, and a smaller class generalizes better. Deep networks complicate this picture. [Zhang et al., 2017] showed that standard convolutional networks fit random labels on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, with and without explicit regularization; weight decay, dropout, and data augmentation improve test accuracy but are neither necessary nor sufficient for generalization. Since then, the field has paid as much attention to implicit regularization (by the optimizer and the architecture) as to explicit penalties. Double descent ([Nakkiran et al., 2020]) and grokking ([Power et al., 2022]) show that the interaction between capacity, data, training time, and regularization is not monotone.

[Kukačka et al., 2017] classify regularization by what it acts on: the data, the network architecture, the error function, the regularization term, and the optimization procedure. This survey uses a similar split, adapted to the generative case:

| Family | Acts on | Representative methods | Section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parameter-norm penalties | weights | L2 / weight decay, L1, max-norm, orthogonality | I.2 |
| Lipschitz and spectral control | weights, architecture | SN, SNR, Parseval, orthogonal and 1-Lipschitz layers | I.3 |
| Input-space smoothness | function | Jacobian and gradient penalties, adversarial training, noise injection | I.4 |
| Stochastic regularization | activations, depth | dropout, DropConnect, DropBlock, stochastic depth | I.5 |
| Normalization and signal propagation | activations, architecture | BN, LN, GN, normalizer-free networks, residual scaling | I.6 |
| Data-side regularization | inputs, targets | augmentation, mixup, CutMix, label smoothing, distillation | I.7 |
| Optimization-side and implicit | training procedure | early stopping, SGD noise, SAM, weight averaging, gradient clipping | I.8 |
| Training recipes | everything | EfficientNet, EfficientNetV2, ConvNeXt, "ResNet strikes back" | I.9 |

The families overlap. Noise injection is a data-side method and an input-smoothness penalty at once (I.4), and batch normalization is an architecture choice with a stochastic regularization effect (I.6). The Lipschitz view in I.3 and I.4 ties several families together, and it carries over to GANs in Part II and to flows in the companion survey. Section I.10 says which families transfer to a GAN critic.

## I.2 Parameter-norm penalties

### L2 regularization and weight decay

The oldest explicit regularizer adds a squared norm of the weights to the loss,

$$\min_\theta \; \mathcal{L}(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \|\theta\|_2^2,$$

which for linear least squares is Tikhonov (ridge) regularization. [Krogh and Hertz, 1991] showed for linear networks that weight decay suppresses irrelevant weight components and part of the noise in the targets. For plain SGD, the L2 penalty and **weight decay**, the update $\theta \leftarrow (1 - \eta \lambda) \theta - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}$, are the same thing.

For adaptive optimizers they are not. Adam rescales the gradient of the penalty by its preconditioner, so weights with large historical gradients are decayed less. [Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019] decoupled the decay from the gradient step (AdamW),

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t \left( \frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t} + \epsilon} + \lambda \theta_t \right),$$

and found that AdamW generalizes better and that its optimal $\lambda$ is less coupled to the learning rate. AdamW is now the default for transformers and modern convolutional networks.

### Weight decay with normalization: an effective learning rate

When a scale-invariant layer follows a weight $w$, the loss satisfies $\mathcal{L}(c w) = \mathcal{L}(w)$, so weight decay cannot change the function. It still changes training. Differentiating $\mathcal{L}(c w) = \mathcal{L}(w)$ gives $c \, \nabla \mathcal{L}(c w) = \nabla \mathcal{L}(w)$, and the gradient is orthogonal to $w$. A step of size $\eta$ therefore rotates $w$ by an angle of about $\eta \|\nabla \mathcal{L}(\hat w)\| / \|w\|^2$, where $\hat w = w / \|w\|$: the effective learning rate on the direction is $\eta / \|w\|^2$. Without weight decay, $\|w\|$ grows with every step (by Pythagoras, since the gradient is orthogonal to $w$), and the effective learning rate decays; weight decay counteracts the growth. [van Laarhoven, 2017] made this argument for L2 with batch normalization, and [Zhang et al., 2019c] identified three mechanisms by which weight decay regularizes: the effective learning rate for normalized layers, an approximate Jacobian-norm regularization for K-FAC, and a change of the curvature for unnormalized networks. [Kosson et al., 2024] showed that weight decay and gradient updates reach a **rotational equilibrium**, where each normalized weight rotates by a fixed expected angle per step, and that this equilibrium explains much of AdamW's advantage over Adam with L2. [Andriushchenko et al., 2023] argued that in modern deep learning weight decay rarely acts as capacity control; it rather shapes optimization dynamics. It raises the effective learning rate (and with it the implicit regularization of SGD noise) for vision models, and it stabilizes the loss and bfloat16 training for language models.

This is directly relevant to the toolkit. The exploration of plan 2026.010 found the same mechanism for a spectral norm penalty: penalizing $\sigma$ of a convolution that feeds a scale-invariant normalization shrinks the weight without changing the function, so the penalty acts as weight decay and raises that layer's effective learning rate (`docs/features/2026.010__efficientnet_arch_mod__1-explore.md`, Section 1.1).

### Scaling weight decay

[Wang and Aitchison, 2025] showed that AdamW's weights are an exponential moving average of recent updates with timescale $\tau_\text{iter} = 1 / (\eta \lambda)$, and found that the optimal timescale measured in epochs stays roughly constant as model and dataset size change. Under this rule the optimal $\lambda$ falls as the dataset grows and rises with model width under muP learning-rate scaling.

### L1, max-norm, and orthogonality

- **L1** ($\lambda \|\theta\|_1$) promotes sparsity. It is rarely used on whole deep networks today, but it remains common for pruning and for sparse codes.
- **Max-norm constraints** project each unit's incoming weight vector onto $\|w\|_2 \le c$ after each step. [Srivastava et al., 2014] found max-norm to combine well with dropout, because it allows large learning rates without exploding weights.
- **Orthogonality regularization** penalizes $\|W^\top W - I\|_F^2$, which pushes all singular values toward 1. [Brock et al., 2017] introduced it for generative models, BigGAN used a relaxed variant (Section II.10), and [Bansal et al., 2018] compared several orthogonality penalties on ResNets and found consistent gains. Orthogonality is a two-sided spectral constraint: it bounds the largest singular value like SN, but it also keeps the smallest singular value from collapsing.

## I.3 Lipschitz and spectral control

### Why the Lipschitz constant

The Lipschitz constant appears in three roles that are often confused:

1. **Generalization.** Margin-based generalization bounds for deep networks scale with the product of the layers' spectral norms ([Bartlett et al., 2017]; [Neyshabur et al., 2018]).
2. **Robustness.** A classifier with margin $m$ and Lipschitz constant $L$ is certifiably robust in a ball of radius $m / (\sqrt{2} L)$ ([Tsuzuku et al., 2018]), the basis of the certified-robustness line of work.
3. **Objectives that require it.** The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual of the Wasserstein-1 distance takes a supremum over 1-Lipschitz functions (Section II.4), and invertible residual flows require each residual branch to be a contraction (companion survey).

For a feed-forward network $f = \phi_L \circ W_L \circ \dots \circ \phi_1 \circ W_1$,

$$\mathrm{Lip}(f) \le \prod_{l=1}^{L} \mathrm{Lip}(\phi_l) \, \sigma(W_l),$$

and for a residual block $x \mapsto x + g(x)$, $\mathrm{Lip} \le 1 + \mathrm{Lip}(g)$. The product bound is cheap but loose: it assumes that the top singular directions of consecutive layers align and that every activation is in its steepest regime at once. Computing the exact Lipschitz constant of a ReLU network is NP-hard ([Virmaux and Scaman, 2018]); semidefinite relaxations such as LipSDP ([Fazlyab et al., 2019]) give tighter certified upper bounds at a high cost. Empirical lower bounds come from the largest input-gradient norm or difference quotient over many points, which is what the toolkit's `examples/nets/efficientnet_empirical_lipschitz.py` reports. [Luo et al., 2026] is a recent systematic review of Lipschitz theory, estimation, regularization, and certification.

### Spectral norm regularization and spectral normalization

[Yoshida and Miyato, 2017] proposed spectral norm regularization, a loss term $\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_l \sigma(W_l)^2$, with $\sigma$ estimated by a few steps of power iteration, and showed improved generalization and lower sensitivity to input perturbations. [Miyato et al., 2018a] turned it into a hard reparametrization,

$$\bar W = \frac{W}{\sigma(W)}, \qquad \tilde v \leftarrow \frac{W^\top \tilde u}{\|W^\top \tilde u\|}, \quad \tilde u \leftarrow \frac{W \tilde v}{\|W \tilde v\|}, \quad \sigma(W) \approx \tilde u^\top W \tilde v,$$

with one power-iteration step per training forward pass and the vectors $\tilde u, \tilde v$ carried across steps. Two properties of this estimate matter in practice:

- $\tilde u^\top W \tilde v \le \sigma(W)$ for unit vectors, so the estimate can only underestimate $\sigma$, and the normalized weight can drift slightly above 1. The toolkit measured this drift (`docs/features/2026.010__efficientnet_arch_mod__3-usage.md`, "What the styles bound").
- Power iteration converges at the rate $(\sigma_2 / \sigma_1)^k$. An orthogonal initialization has $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$, so the estimate converges slowly at the start of training, which is why `set_init_parameters` re-syncs the vectors after the initialization.

Spectral normalization became the default discriminator regularizer for GANs (Section II.6). For classification, [Gouk et al., 2021] studied Lipschitz constraints as a regularizer, with per-layer projections for several $p$-norms, and found that they improve generalization, especially with little data.

**Parseval networks** ([Cisse et al., 2017]) keep each weight matrix approximately orthonormal with a retraction step after each update, so every layer has $\sigma \le 1$, and use convex combinations in the residual sums. They were introduced for adversarial robustness.

### The operator norm of a convolution

A convolution is a linear operator, but its operator norm is not the spectral norm of the kernel reshaped into a matrix, which is what PyTorch's `spectral_norm` uses. [Sedghi et al., 2019] showed that for circular padding the singular values of a multi-channel 2D convolution are the singular values of the per-frequency $C_\text{out} \times C_\text{in}$ matrices $\hat K(\omega)$ of the kernel's discrete Fourier transform, so

$$\|K\|_\text{op} = \max_\omega \sigma_\text{max}\big(\hat K(\omega)\big),$$

and gave a method to project onto an operator-norm ball. The reshaped-matrix norm can underestimate the operator norm by up to a factor $\sqrt{k}$ for kernel size $k$ ([Singla and Feizi, 2019]; the toolkit relies on this bound). A one-channel example shows the gap: the averaging kernel $(1/k, \dots, 1/k)$ has reshaped-matrix norm $1/\sqrt{k}$ but operator norm 1, because a constant signal passes through unchanged. Several methods address this:

- **Power iteration on the operator** ([Farnia et al., 2019]): alternate convolution and transposed convolution instead of multiplying with the reshaped matrix. Exact for any padding, but it costs a forward and a backward convolution per step.
- **Fantastic Four** ([Singla and Feizi, 2019]): a differentiable upper bound from four reshapings of the kernel, at most $\sqrt{hw}$ times the true norm.
- **Gram iteration** ([Delattre et al., 2023]): a fast, differentiable, certified upper bound on the spectral norm of circular convolutions that converges super-linearly, and its extension to zero padding ([Delattre et al., 2024]).

The toolkit's `DepthwiseSpectralNorm` uses the Fourier view for depthwise 1D convolutions, where every per-frequency matrix is diagonal, so the operator norm is the largest DFT magnitude of any channel's filter (Part IV).

### Lipschitz-by-construction layers

A separate line of work builds layers that are 1-Lipschitz by construction, mostly for certified robustness:

- **GroupSort and MaxMin activations** ([Anil et al., 2019]) are gradient-norm preserving. With norm-constrained weights and ReLU, a network cannot represent some 1-Lipschitz functions with gradient norm 1 almost everywhere, which is what an optimal Wasserstein critic needs; GroupSort fixes this.
- **Orthogonal convolutions**: block convolution orthogonal parametrization (BCOP, [Li et al., 2019a]), the Cayley transform in the Fourier domain ([Trockman and Kolter, 2021]), skew orthogonal convolutions (SOC, [Singla and Feizi, 2021]), and layer-wise orthogonal training (LOT, [Xu et al., 2022]).
- **Rescaling methods**: almost-orthogonal layers (AOL, [Prach and Lampert, 2022]) rescale $W$ by a diagonal matrix from $|W^\top W|$, certified 1-Lipschitz without iteration; SDP-based Lipschitz layers (SLL, [Araujo et al., 2023]) give a residual 1-Lipschitz layer and a unifying semidefinite view of SN, AOL, orthogonal, and convex-potential layers.
- **Scaling up**: LiResNet ([Hu et al., 2023]) trains 1-Lipschitz residual networks without batch normalization on ImageNet. [Prach et al., 2024] benchmark 1-Lipschitz layers by memory, speed, and certified accuracy and find large differences in cost for similar accuracy.

These layers give more than a GAN critic needs. They matter here as references for single components: an activation (GroupSort), a normalization replacement, or an exact convolution norm.

## I.4 Input-space smoothness

### Jacobian and gradient penalties

Instead of bounding the weights, one can penalize the function's sensitivity to its input directly. **Double backpropagation** ([Drucker and LeCun, 1992]) adds $\lambda \|\nabla_x \mathcal{L}\|^2$ to the loss. [Sokolić et al., 2017] related the Frobenius norm of the input–output Jacobian to the classification margin and proposed it as a regularizer, and [Novak et al., 2018] found empirically that the Jacobian norm at test points correlates well with generalization. [Hoffman et al., 2019] made Jacobian regularization cheap with random projections,

$$\|J_f(x)\|_F^2 = C \, \mathbb{E}_{v \sim \mathcal{U}(S^{C-1})} \|v^\top J_f(x)\|^2,$$

estimated with one or a few vector–Jacobian products. Gradient penalties have a practical cost that recurs throughout Part II: their gradient with respect to $\theta$ needs a second backward pass ("double backward"), which some compilers and fused kernels do not support.

### Adversarial training and virtual adversarial training

Adversarial training ([Goodfellow et al., 2015]; [Madry et al., 2018]) minimizes the loss at worst-case perturbations $\max_{\|\delta\| \le \epsilon} \mathcal{L}(x + \delta)$. To first order, it penalizes the dual norm of the input gradient. **Virtual adversarial training** ([Miyato et al., 2018b]) penalizes the KL divergence between predictions at $x$ and at $x + r_\text{adv}$, where $r_\text{adv}$ is found by one step of power iteration on the Hessian of that divergence. It needs no labels, so it also works as a semi-supervised smoothness regularizer. The same power-iteration idea reappears in the adversarial Lipschitz penalty for GAN critics (Section II.4).

### Noise injection is Tikhonov regularization

[Bishop, 1995] showed that training with small additive input noise $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ is, to second order, equivalent to a Tikhonov regularizer on the input derivatives. For a squared loss, expand $f(x + \xi) = f(x) + J_f(x) \xi + \frac{1}{2} \xi^\top \nabla^2 f(x) \xi + \dots$; the first-order term has zero mean, and its square contributes $\mathbb{E} \|J_f \xi\|^2 = \sigma^2 \|J_f\|_F^2$:

$$\mathbb{E}_\xi \, \|f(x + \xi) - y\|^2 = \|f(x) - y\|^2 + \sigma^2 \, \|J_f(x)\|_F^2 + \sigma^2 \, (f(x) - y)^\top \Delta f(x) + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^4).$$

The last term, with the Laplacian $\Delta f$, has no fixed sign, but it vanishes at the optimum of the regression, where the residual has zero conditional mean. This equivalence links noise-based methods (instance noise, consistency regularization, approximated gradient penalties) to explicit gradient penalties; it returns in Sections II.5, II.8, and III.3. The toolkit's survey on feature noise ([[2025.003__feature_noise__1-survey]]) covers noise injection inside networks.

## I.5 Stochastic regularization

**Dropout** ([Srivastava et al., 2014]) multiplies hidden units by Bernoulli masks during training, $\tilde h = m \odot h / (1 - p)$ with $m_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1 - p)$, and uses the full network at test time. It can be read as training an exponential ensemble of subnetworks with shared weights, as approximate Bayesian inference ([Gal and Ghahramani, 2016]), or, for linear models, as a data-dependent L2 penalty. Variants differ in what they drop:

- **DropConnect** ([Wan et al., 2013]) drops weights instead of units.
- **SpatialDropout** ([Tompson et al., 2015]) drops whole feature maps, because neighboring activations in a convolutional feature map are correlated and element-wise dropout barely removes information.
- **DropBlock** ([Ghiasi et al., 2018]) drops contiguous regions, for the same reason.
- **Stochastic depth** ([Huang et al., 2016]), also called drop path, drops whole residual branches per sample: $x_{l+1} = x_l + b_l f_l(x_l) / p_l$ with $b_l \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_l)$. It regularizes and also shortens the expected depth during training. EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, and vision transformers all use it, with a drop rate that grows linearly with depth.
- **Shake-Shake** ([Gastaldi, 2017]) mixes two residual branches with random weights that differ in the forward and backward pass.

Dropout and batch normalization interact badly: dropout shifts the variance of activations between training and inference, which BN's running statistics do not see ([Li et al., 2019b]). [Liu et al., 2023] found that dropout applied only early in training reduces underfitting of large models, by aligning mini-batch gradients with the full-data gradient, a use of dropout that does not regularize in the classic sense.

For GANs, stochastic regularization of the discriminator is rarely used, for a reason the toolkit measured: a random critic makes the evaluations within one gradient or finite-difference penalty see different networks (Section II.14).

## I.6 Normalization and signal propagation

Normalization layers were introduced to speed up optimization, but they also regularize. **Batch normalization** ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015]) normalizes with mini-batch statistics, which injects sample-dependent noise; [Luo et al., 2019] analyzed this noise as an explicit regularizer on the scale of the weights and the gamma parameters. [Santurkar et al., 2018] argued that BN works by smoothing the loss landscape rather than by reducing internal covariate shift. Batch-independent alternatives normalize per sample: **layer normalization** ([Ba et al., 2016]), **group normalization** ([Wu and He, 2018]), weight normalization ([Salimans and Kingma, 2016]), weight standardization ([Qiao et al., 2019]), filter response normalization ([Singh and Krishnan, 2020]), and EvoNorm ([Liu et al., 2020]), the last evaluated on EfficientNets. Proxy normalization ([Labatie et al., 2021]) recovers BN accuracy with GN or LN, and [Masters et al., 2021] applied it to EfficientNet.

**Normalizer-free residual networks** replace normalization by initialization and residual scaling: Fixup ([Zhang et al., 2019a]), SkipInit ([De and Smith, 2020]), and ReZero ([Bachlechner et al., 2021]) initialize the residual branch to zero, $x + \alpha f(x)$ with $\alpha = 0$ at the start. NF-ResNets ([Brock et al., 2021a]) use $x_{l+1} = x_l + \alpha f_l(x_l / \beta_l)$, with $\beta_l$ the expected standard deviation of $x_l$, and scaled weight standardization. They introduced **signal propagation plots** to diagnose the scale of activations through depth. NFNets ([Brock et al., 2021b]) add **adaptive gradient clipping** (AGC), which clips each unit's gradient when its norm exceeds a fraction of the unit's weight norm, and match the accuracy of batch-normalized EfficientNets at lower training cost. ConvNeXt ([Liu et al., 2022]) uses one LayerNorm per inverted-bottleneck block and a learnable per-channel residual scale (LayerScale, [Touvron et al., 2021]).

Two facts matter for regularization in GANs:

1. **Scale invariance cancels weight constraints.** Every normalization that divides by a statistic of its input is scale invariant, so it removes the effect of SN or SNR on the layers before it (Section I.2 and the toolkit's 2026.010 exploration). Lipschitz-aware normalizations avoid this: CenterNorm ([Qi et al., 2023]) only centers and is Lipschitz, and constrained BN ([Gouk et al., 2021]) clips the eval-mode gain $|\gamma_c| / \sqrt{\mathrm{var}_c + \epsilon}$.
2. **Batch coupling breaks per-sample penalties.** A batch-normalized critic maps a batch to a batch, so the per-sample gradient penalty is no longer the gradient of the function being constrained ([Gulrajani et al., 2017]). BN also increases sensitivity to input perturbations ([Galloway et al., 2019]).

The toolkit's analysis organizes normalizations along these two axes, batch coupled or per sample, and scale invariant or not. Only per-sample normalizations that are not scale invariant (CenterNorm, affine-only, no normalization, or a normalization with a floored denominator) serve a critic that is both penalized per sample and spectrally normalized.

## I.7 Data-side regularization

**Data augmentation** is the most effective regularizer for image classifiers. Classic geometric and color transforms were complemented by:

- **Cutout** ([DeVries and Taylor, 2017]) and **random erasing** ([Zhong et al., 2020]): mask a random rectangle.
- **Mixup** ([Zhang et al., 2018a]): train on convex combinations $\tilde x = \lambda x_i + (1 - \lambda) x_j$, $\tilde y = \lambda y_i + (1 - \lambda) y_j$ with $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$, which encourages linear behavior between training points.
- **CutMix** ([Yun et al., 2019]): paste a patch of one image into another and mix the labels by area.
- **Learned and random policies**: AutoAugment ([Cubuk et al., 2019]) searches augmentation policies, and RandAugment ([Cubuk et al., 2020]) replaces the search by two parameters, the number of transforms and a shared magnitude.

**Label smoothing** ([Szegedy et al., 2016]) trains on $y^\text{LS} = (1 - \alpha) y + \alpha / K$, which keeps logits finite and improves calibration; it also erases information in the penultimate layer that knowledge distillation would use ([Müller et al., 2019]). **Knowledge distillation** ([Hinton et al., 2015]) trains on a teacher's soft predictions, which regularizes by transferring the teacher's similarity structure between classes.

In GANs, augmentation must be applied with care, because the discriminator can learn the augmentations and the generator then reproduces them (leaking, Section II.8).

## I.8 Optimization-side and implicit regularization

- **Early stopping** limits the effective capacity reached by gradient descent; for linear models with squared loss it behaves like L2 regularization with a strength set by the stopping time.
- **SGD noise.** Small-batch SGD tends to find flatter minima that generalize better ([Keskar et al., 2017]), and the relevant quantity is the noise scale, about $\eta N / B$ for learning rate $\eta$, training-set size $N$, and batch size $B$ ([Smith and Le, 2018]).
- **Implicit gradient regularization.** Backward error analysis shows that gradient descent with finite step size $\eta$ follows the gradient flow of a modified loss $\tilde{\mathcal{L}} = \mathcal{L} + \frac{\eta}{4} \|\nabla \mathcal{L}\|^2$ ([Barrett and Dherin, 2021]); for SGD, an additional term penalizes the norms of the mini-batch gradients ([Smith et al., 2021]).
- **Sharpness-aware minimization** (SAM, [Foret et al., 2021]) solves $\min_\theta \max_{\|\epsilon\| \le \rho} \mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon)$, approximated by one ascent step $\hat\epsilon = \rho \nabla \mathcal{L} / \|\nabla \mathcal{L}\|$, at the cost of a second gradient evaluation.
- **Weight averaging.** Polyak–Ruppert averaging ([Polyak and Juditsky, 1992]), the exponential moving average $\bar\theta_t = \beta \bar\theta_{t-1} + (1 - \beta) \theta_t$, mean teachers ([Tarvainen and Valpola, 2017]), and stochastic weight averaging (SWA, [Izmailov et al., 2018]) all evaluate an average of iterates, which lies in a flatter region than the last iterate. EMA of generator weights is standard for GANs (Section II.10).
- **Gradient clipping** ([Pascanu et al., 2013]) bounds the update norm and prevents rare large steps; AGC (Section I.6) is a per-unit variant.

## I.9 Training recipes for convolutional networks, and EfficientNet in particular

Several studies found that the regularization recipe matters as much as the architecture. [Bello et al., 2021] showed that a ResNet with modern training and regularization (label smoothing, dropout, stochastic depth, RandAugment, decreased weight decay) closes most of the gap to EfficientNet. [Wightman et al., 2021] ("ResNet strikes back") reached 80.4% ImageNet top-1 with a ResNet-50 through the training procedure alone.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| EfficientNetV2 ([Tan and Le, 2021]) | RMSProp | dropout, ramped per stage | RandAugment and mixup, ramped per stage | progressive resizing |

**EfficientNet** raises depth, width, and resolution together; larger models need more regularization, so the dropout rate grows with the model.

**EfficientNetV2** introduced **progressive learning with adaptive regularization**: training starts with small images and weak regularization and raises both the image size and the regularization strength (dropout rate, RandAugment magnitude, mixup ratio) stage by stage. The paper's argument is that small images need weak regularization and large images need strong regularization, so a fixed strength is wrong at some stage of progressive resizing. This is an early example of a regularization schedule, an idea that GANs use with adaptive augmentation strengths (Section II.8).

# Part II: Regularization of GANs

## II.1 Why GAN discriminators need regularization

A GAN ([Goodfellow et al., 2014]) trains a generator $G$ and a discriminator $D$ in a two-player game,

$$\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_r} [\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z} [\log(1 - D(G(z)))].$$

For an optimal $D$, the generator minimizes the Jensen–Shannon divergence between $P_r$ and $P_g$. Regularization addresses four distinct problems, and a method that solves one need not solve the others:

1. **Vanishing or uninformative gradients.** If $P_r$ and $P_g$ lie on disjoint low-dimensional manifolds, a perfect discriminator exists, the Jensen–Shannon divergence is constant, and its gradient carries no information ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017]). Remedies are divergences that remain informative (Wasserstein, Section II.4), smoothness constraints on $D$, and noise that makes the supports overlap (Section II.8).
2. **Non-convergence of the game dynamics.** Simultaneous or alternating gradient descent on a two-player game need not converge, even locally. Section II.2 works through the smallest example. [Mescheder et al., 2017] and [Nagarajan and Kolter, 2017] gave related analyses based on the eigenvalues of the game's Jacobian.
3. **Discriminator overfitting.** With limited data, $D$ memorizes the training set: its outputs on real and generated data separate, its feedback to $G$ becomes meaningless, and FID degrades as training continues ([Karras et al., 2020b]). Remedies are augmentation and consistency regularization (Section II.8).
4. **Generalization of the learned distribution.** A discriminator with finite capacity measures a weaker "neural net distance" that can be small even if $G$ has collapsed ([Arora et al., 2017]); restricting the discriminator class trades discrimination against generalization ([Zhang et al., 2018b]). [Thanh-Tung et al., 2019] linked poor generalization and mode collapse to gradient explosion of $D$ near training samples, and showed that a zero-centered gradient penalty on interpolates helps.

Two broad references cover the GAN regularization literature: [Li et al., 2023] organize regularization and normalization methods by the problem they address (Lipschitz, data augmentation, consistency, self-supervision), and the large-scale study of [Kurach et al., 2019] compares losses, normalizations, and penalties empirically (Section II.12).

## II.2 A worked example: the Dirac-GAN

[Mescheder et al., 2018] analyzed the simplest GAN that still fails. The data distribution is a point mass at 0, $P_r = \delta_0$; the generator is a point mass at $\theta$, $P_g = \delta_\theta$; the discriminator is linear, $D_\psi(x) = \psi x$. With a loss $f$ applied to the discriminator's output (for the non-saturating GAN, $f(t) = -\log(1 + e^{-t})$), the game is

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta, \psi) = f(\psi \theta) + f(0),$$

which $D$ maximizes over $\psi$ and $G$ minimizes over $\theta$. The unique equilibrium is $(\theta, \psi) = (0, 0)$. The gradient vector field of the game, with $G$ descending and $D$ ascending, is

$$v(\theta, \psi) = \begin{pmatrix} -f'(\psi \theta) \, \psi \\ f'(\psi \theta) \, \theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nabla v(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -f'(0) \\ f'(0) & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The Jacobian at the equilibrium has eigenvalues $\pm i f'(0)$, purely imaginary. Continuous-time gradient flow circles the equilibrium forever, simultaneous gradient descent spirals outward (every step multiplies the distance by $\sqrt{1 + \eta^2 f'(0)^2} > 1$), and alternating gradient descent stays on a bounded orbit without converging. The training never settles: $D$ keeps pushing $G$ past the data and back.

**$R_1$ fixes it.** The discriminator's input gradient is $\nabla_x D_\psi = \psi$, so $R_1 = \frac{\gamma}{2} \psi^2$ at the data point. Adding it to the discriminator's objective (as a penalty in its maximization) adds $-\gamma \psi$ to the $\psi$-component of the vector field, and the Jacobian becomes

$$\nabla v(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -f'(0) \\ f'(0) & -\gamma \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{-\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - 4 f'(0)^2}}{2}.$$

Both eigenvalues have negative real part for every $\gamma > 0$, so the equilibrium is locally attracting, and gradient descent with a small enough step converges. The penalty damps the oscillation by making $D$ forget its slope when $G$ is already on the data.

**Other methods on the same example.** Mescheder et al. showed that instance noise (Section II.8) also adds damping and converges, that WGAN with a finite number of critic steps does not converge (the clipped linear critic hops between $\pm c$), and that WGAN-GP does not converge in general, because its penalty pulls the slope toward 1, not 0: at the equilibrium the optimal $D$ should be flat on the data. This one example motivates the zero-centered penalties of Section II.5.

## II.3 GAN losses in brief

Regularizers are added to a loss, and some only make sense for certain losses. The toolkit implements three of the following (Part IV).

| Loss | Discriminator objective (maximize) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-saturating (NS) ([Goodfellow et al., 2014]) | $\mathbb{E}_{P_r} \log D + \mathbb{E}_{P_g} \log(1 - D)$ | $G$ maximizes $\log D(G(z))$ instead of minimizing $\log(1 - D)$ |
| Least squares (LSGAN) ([Mao et al., 2017]) | $-\mathbb{E}_{P_r} (D - 1)^2 - \mathbb{E}_{P_g} D^2$ | Pearson $\chi^2$ for suitable targets |
| Hinge ([Lim and Ye, 2017]; [Miyato et al., 2018a]) | $\mathbb{E}_{P_r} \min(0, D - 1) + \mathbb{E}_{P_g} \min(0, -D - 1)$ | default with SN in SNGAN, SAGAN, BigGAN |
| Wasserstein (WGAN) ([Arjovsky et al., 2017]) | $\mathbb{E}_{P_r} D - \mathbb{E}_{P_g} D$, $D$ 1-Lipschitz | needs a Lipschitz constraint (Section II.4) |
| Relativistic pairing (RpGAN) ([Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2019]) | $\mathbb{E}_{x, z} f(D(x) - D(G(z)))$ | compares pairs; basis of R3GAN and GAT (Section II.5) |

The Wasserstein loss is special: without a Lipschitz constraint the critic's objective is unbounded, so the constraint is part of the loss's definition, not an add-on. The other losses are bounded for a fixed $G$, so their regularizers aim at convergence and generalization rather than well-posedness. In practice this decides the penalty: one-centered or one-sided penalties for Wasserstein critics, zero-centered penalties for the others.

## II.4 Lipschitz constraints for Wasserstein critics

### The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual

The Wasserstein-1 distance has the dual form

$$W_1(P_r, P_g) = \sup_{\|D\|_L \le 1} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_r}[D(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_g}[D(x)].$$

The original WGAN ([Arjovsky et al., 2017]) enforced the constraint by **weight clipping** to $[-c, c]$. Clipping bounds the Lipschitz constant only loosely, biases the critic toward simple functions, and leads to vanishing or exploding gradients depending on $c$.

### WGAN-GP: the one-centered gradient penalty

[Gulrajani et al., 2017] proved that, for an optimal critic $D^*$ and an optimal coupling $\pi$ of $P_r$ and $P_g$, the critic is linear with slope 1 along every transport segment: for $(x, y) \sim \pi$ and $x_t = t x + (1 - t) y$, $\nabla D^*(x_t) = (x - x_t) / \|x - x_t\|$, so $\|\nabla D^*(x_t)\| = 1$. They penalized the gradient norm at random interpolates,

$$\mathcal{R}_\text{GP} = \lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{\hat x} \big(\|\nabla_x D(\hat x)\| - 1\big)^2, \qquad \hat x = t x_r + (1 - t) x_g, \quad t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1),$$

with $\lambda = 10$, five critic steps per generator step, Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$ and $\beta_2 = 0.9$, and no batch normalization in the critic (layer normalization instead). WGAN-GP made Wasserstein GANs practical and became the most cited GAN regularizer. Its known weaknesses:

- The interpolates pair random real and generated samples, not samples coupled by the optimal plan, so the unit-gradient target holds only approximately. Several papers showed that the penalty biases the critic and the estimated distance ([Petzka et al., 2018]; [Zhou et al., 2019b]).
- The two-sided form forces slope 1 where the optimal critic may be flatter, and it does not converge on the Dirac-GAN (Section II.2).
- It needs a double backward pass.

### One-sided, consistency, and local variants

- **WGAN-LP** ([Petzka et al., 2018]): the one-sided penalty $\lambda \, \mathbb{E} \max(0, \|\nabla D(\hat x)\| - 1)^2$, which enforces the Lipschitz constraint as an inequality, as the dual requires. The paper argues that the two-sided penalty is not justified by the theory and shows more stable training with the one-sided form, especially at large $\lambda$.
- **CT-GAN** ([Wei et al., 2018]): adds a consistency term that penalizes difference quotients between two dropout-perturbed critic evaluations near each real sample, $\mathbb{E} \max(0, d(D(x'), D(x'')) / d(x', x'') - M')$. The term enforces the Lipschitz condition near the data manifold, where the interpolates of WGAN-GP rarely land.
- **DRAGAN** ([Kodali et al., 2017]): penalizes $\lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_r, \delta} (\|\nabla D(x + \delta)\| - k)^2$ only in a noisy neighborhood of the real data, motivated by a no-regret analysis that attributes mode collapse to sharp gradients of $D$ around real points.
- **WGAN-div** ([Wu et al., 2018]): a penalty $k \, \mathbb{E} \|\nabla D\|^p$ that defines a valid divergence without a Lipschitz constraint, evaluated on real and generated samples.
- **Banach WGAN** ([Adler and Lunz, 2018]): the gradient penalty in the dual norm of a Banach space (for example Sobolev norms), so the critic measures distances in the chosen norm. For time series, this is the place to choose a norm that weights frequencies or derivatives.
- **Integral probability metrics with other constraints**: Fisher GAN ([Mroueh and Sercu, 2017]) constrains the second moment of the critic, and Sobolev GAN ([Mroueh et al., 2018]) constrains the expected squared gradient norm under a reference measure.
- **Lipschitz GANs** ([Zhou et al., 2019a]) showed that a Lipschitz constraint on the discriminator gives informative gradients for a large family of losses, not only the Wasserstein loss, and proposed a max-gradient-norm penalty.

### Adversarial Lipschitz regularization

[Terjék, 2020] replaced the random interpolate by an adversarial direction. Starting from a random perturbation, one step of power iteration (as in virtual adversarial training) finds the direction $r_\text{adv}$ in which the critic changes most, and the penalty acts on the difference quotient,

$$\mathcal{R}_\text{ALP} = \lambda \, \mathbb{E} \max\left(0, \frac{|D(x) - D(x + r_\text{adv})|}{\|r_\text{adv}\|} - K\right)^2.$$

The method needs only first-order gradients for the direction search and forward passes for the penalty, and it penalizes the Lipschitz constraint where it is most violated. It is the closest published method to the toolkit's `gradient_penalty_fd_adversarial`.

### Gradient normalization

Instead of a penalty, **gradient normalization** divides the network by its gradient norm: GN-GAN ([Wu et al., 2021]) uses $\hat D(x) = D(x) / (\|\nabla_x D(x)\| + |D(x)|)$, which is piecewise $K$-Lipschitz, and GraN-GAN ([Bhaskara et al., 2022]) normalizes piecewise-linear discriminators by their input gradient. Both need a double backward pass in training, as the gradient penalty does.

### GroupSort critics

With spectrally normalized weights and ReLU, a critic cannot reach gradient norm 1 everywhere, so it underestimates $W_1$; gradient-norm-preserving activations such as GroupSort fix this ([Anil et al., 2019]). This is the only line of work in this section that changes the activation rather than the weights or the loss.

## II.5 Zero-centered gradient penalties and convergence

### From noise to gradient penalties

[Roth et al., 2017] derived a gradient penalty for $f$-GANs from noise. Convolving both distributions with Gaussian noise and expanding to second order gives, for the Jensen–Shannon GAN with $D = \mathrm{sigmoid}(\phi)$,

$$\Omega_\text{JS} = \mathbb{E}_{P_r}\big[(1 - D(x))^2 \|\nabla \phi(x)\|^2\big] + \mathbb{E}_{P_g}\big[D(x)^2 \|\nabla \phi(x)\|^2\big],$$

a zero-centered penalty weighted by how wrong $D$ is: real points the discriminator already classifies as real ($D \approx 1$) and fake points it already rejects ($D \approx 0$) are barely penalized. The derivation is Bishop's noise–Tikhonov equivalence (Section I.4) applied to the discriminator.

### R1 and R2

[Mescheder et al., 2018] simplified this to unweighted penalties on the real or the generated distribution,

$$R_1 = \frac{\gamma}{2} \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_r} \|\nabla_x D(x)\|^2, \qquad R_2 = \frac{\gamma}{2} \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_g} \|\nabla_x D(x)\|^2,$$

and proved that both give local convergence of GAN training for absolutely continuous and for some singular distributions (Section II.2 for the simplest case), while WGAN-GP and unregularized training need not converge. $R_1$ with the non-saturating loss became the standard in high-quality image GANs: StyleGAN ([Karras et al., 2019]) and StyleGAN2 ([Karras et al., 2020a]), with $\gamma$ tuned per dataset and resolution. StyleGAN2-ADA gives a heuristic starting value, $\gamma_0 = 0.0002 \cdot N / M$ for $N$ pixels and batch size $M$ ([Karras et al., 2020b]); the scaling with $N$ reflects that $\|\nabla_x D\|^2$ sums over all input dimensions.

[Thanh-Tung et al., 2019] compared zero- and one-centered penalties on interpolates. They showed that the zero-centered version improves generalization and reduces mode collapse, because it bounds the gradient of $D$ near training samples and keeps it from memorizing them.

### Lazy regularization

StyleGAN2 introduced **lazy regularization**: compute the $R_1$ term only every 16 minibatches and scale it by 16. Gradient penalties are expensive and their effect changes slowly, so this cuts the cost with no loss of quality ([Karras et al., 2020a]). With Adam, the authors also adjust the optimizer's learning rate and betas for the regularized steps so the effective dynamics match. The same applies to the generator's path-length regularizer (every 4 minibatches, Section II.10).

### R3GAN: R1 and R2 together with a relativistic loss

[Huang et al., 2024] ("The GAN is dead; long live the GAN!") revisited the regularization of modern GANs. Their loss is the relativistic pairing loss with both zero-centered penalties,

$$\mathcal{L}_D = \mathbb{E}_{z, x} \big[f\big(D(G(z)) - D(x)\big)\big] + R_1 + R_2, \qquad f(t) = -\log(1 + e^{-t}),$$

written as the discriminator's minimization objective. They argue and show empirically that $R_1$ alone fails with the relativistic loss: early in training the parameters are far from the equilibrium where the local convergence proof applies, and with a strong discriminator the penalty on real data alone leaves the discriminator ill-behaved on fake data. On Stacked MNIST, RpGAN with $R_1$ alone diverged for every $\gamma$ from 0.1 to 100. With both penalties, the gradient norms on real and fake data become about equal, and the discriminator behaves like a maximum-margin classifier. The stable loss allowed them to remove most of StyleGAN2's tricks (minibatch standard deviation, equalized learning rate, the mapping network, path-length regularization, lazy regularization, all normalization layers) and to train a ConvNeXt-style generator and discriminator with Adam $\beta_1 = 0$. R3GAN outperforms StyleGAN2 on FFHQ, ImageNet, CIFAR-10, and Stacked MNIST. It is the current reference for a GAN with few tricks, and its regularization is just two zero-centered gradient penalties.

### Approximated R1 without double backward

Large transformer discriminators are trained with fully sharded data parallelism, gradient checkpointing, and fused attention kernels, none of which support double backward. **Adversarial post-training** (APT, [Lin et al., 2025]) for one-step video generation therefore replaced $R_1$ by a finite difference on Gaussian-perturbed real data,

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{aR1} = \lambda \, \mathbb{E}_x \big\| D(x) - D(x + \sigma \xi) \big\|^2, \qquad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),$$

with $\lambda = 100$ and a small noise scale (reported as 0.01 for images and 0.1 for videos), applied on every discriminator step. The authors report that without it the discriminator loss quickly approaches zero and the generator collapses into flat color fields.

**Why it approximates $R_1$.** Expand $D(x + \sigma \xi) - D(x) = \sigma g^\top \xi + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \xi^\top H \xi + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^3)$ with $g = \nabla_x D(x)$ and $H = \nabla_x^2 D(x)$. Squaring and taking the expectation over $\xi$: the first-order term gives $\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}(g^\top \xi)^2 = \sigma^2 \|g\|^2$; the cross term $\sigma^3 \mathbb{E}[(g^\top \xi)(\xi^\top H \xi)]$ vanishes, because odd moments of a symmetric Gaussian are zero; the next term is of order $\sigma^4$ and involves the Hessian, $\frac{\sigma^4}{4} \mathbb{E}(\xi^\top H \xi)^2 = \frac{\sigma^4}{4} \big((\mathrm{tr} H)^2 + 2 \|H\|_F^2\big)$, plus a term from the third derivative of the same order. So

$$\mathbb{E}_\xi \big(D(x + \sigma \xi) - D(x)\big)^2 = \sigma^2 \|\nabla_x D(x)\|^2 + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^4),$$

and the approximated penalty is $R_1$ with $\gamma \approx 2 \lambda \sigma^2$, plus a curvature penalty that grows with $\sigma$. For APT's image setting, $\gamma \approx 2 \cdot 100 \cdot 10^{-4} = 0.02$. The bias is a feature in moderation: the $\sigma^4$ term penalizes the discriminator's curvature, a smoothness regularizer of its own. The variance is the cost: one Gaussian draw per sample estimates $\|g\|^2$ with relative variance about 2, since $(g^\top \xi)^2 / \|g\|^2$ is $\chi^2_1$ distributed.

**GAT** ([Hyun et al., 2025]) combines this with R3GAN's recipe: a relativistic pairing loss with both an approximated $R_1$ on real and an approximated $R_2$ on generated latents, each a squared difference of discriminator outputs under noise scaled by $1 / \sigma^2$, with $\sigma = 0.01$ and weight $0.1$, for transformer generators and discriminators in a VAE latent space. It adds a width-aware learning-rate rule, $\eta = \eta_\text{base} \, C_\text{base} / C_\text{model}$ for channel count $C$, and reports an FID of 2.18 for one-step class-conditional ImageNet-256 generation after 60 epochs. Dividing by $\sigma^2$ makes the weight comparable to $\gamma$, independent of the noise scale, a convention worth adopting.

A 2026 paper on diffusion transformer super-resolution uses the same approximation ([Wang et al., 2026]). The toolkit's finite-difference penalties were designed for the same reason, `torch.compile`'s lack of double backward support, and the approximated $R_1$ is a variant they do not yet cover (Part IV).

### Gradient penalties in adversarial distillation

Adversarial diffusion distillation (ADD, [Sauer et al., 2023]) uses a hinge loss with $R_1$ computed on the input features of each discriminator head instead of on pixels, and reports that it helps most above $128^2$ pixels. Diffusion2GAN ([Kang et al., 2024]) uses a single-sample $R_1$ on a diffusion-initialized discriminator. One-step generators are where gradient penalties are most actively developed today.

### Recent variations

- The **$\varepsilon$-centered gradient penalty** of [Wan et al., 2025] (Li-CFG) enlarges the discriminator's gradient norm with a hyperparameter $\varepsilon$ instead of shrinking it, motivated by a functional-gradient view of GAN training; the authors report better diversity and leave open why it works.
- **One-Forcing** ([Feng et al., 2026]), a one-step autoregressive video generator that reuses the score network as discriminator, reports that its frame-wise variant trains stably without $R_1$/$R_2$, while its chunk-wise variant needs them.

## II.6 Spectral normalization and weight-based Lipschitz control

### SNGAN and its adoption

SNGAN ([Miyato et al., 2018a]) applied spectral normalization to every layer of the discriminator, with no other normalization and the hinge loss, and produced the first high-quality class-conditional ImageNet samples from a single GAN. SN has three practical advantages over gradient penalties: it costs one matrix–vector product per layer and step, it needs no double backward, and it constrains the discriminator everywhere, not only at sampled points. SAGAN ([Zhang et al., 2019b]) applied SN to the generator as well and paired it with the two time-scale update rule (TTUR, [Heusel et al., 2017]), with a learning rate of $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for $D$ and $10^{-4}$ for $G$, in place of several discriminator steps per generator step. BigGAN ([Brock et al., 2019]) kept SN in both networks and scaled to batch size 2048.

### Why spectral normalization helps

[Lin et al., 2021] analyzed SN through the lens of exploding and vanishing gradients. SN controls exploding gradients by bounding each layer, and at initialization it behaves like LeCun initialization, which avoids vanishing gradients; its scale, however, makes activations shrink with depth in networks without normalization. They proposed **bidirectional scaled spectral normalization** (BSSN), which normalizes with the average of the forward and backward spectral norms and multiplies by a constant that compensates the shrinkage. The toolkit met the same shrinkage in its spectrally normalized EfficientNet presets: the stream drops to about $10^{-4}$ at the first MBConv block without residual. It addressed the shrinkage with a floored pre-normalization (Part IV).

### Spectral collapse

[Liu et al., 2019] observed that mode collapse in GANs is preceded by a **spectral collapse**: the singular values of the discriminator's weight matrices fall sharply, except the largest. SN does not prevent this, and the published SNR form $\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_l \sigma_l^2$ shrinks every layer, including layers already below 1. Their **spectral regularization** compensates the spectral distribution of each layer so that the smaller singular values do not collapse. This is the reason the toolkit's `SpectralNormPenalty` defaults to a hinge form that penalizes only spectral norms above a target.

### Equalized learning rate and other reparametrizations

ProGAN ([Karras et al., 2018]) introduced the **equalized learning rate**: initialize weights from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and multiply by the He constant at run time, so every layer learns at the same speed under Adam. It is a reparametrization, not a constraint, but it interacts with regularization: weight decay and gradient penalties act on the stored weights, not on the scaled ones. ProGAN also added a **drift penalty** $\epsilon_\text{drift} \, \mathbb{E}_{P_r} D(x)^2$ with $\epsilon_\text{drift} = 0.001$ to keep the WGAN-GP critic's outputs near zero; the Wasserstein loss is invariant to adding a constant to $D$, so without it the outputs can drift. Sparsity-aware normalization (SAN, [Kligvasser and Michaeli, 2021]) argues that SN over-restricts the discriminator and replaces it with a normalization tuned to sparse feature maps.

### Normalization inside discriminators

Most strong discriminators use no batch normalization. WGAN-GP replaced BN by layer normalization, SNGAN and BigGAN use no normalization in $D$, StyleGAN2 uses none but adds a **minibatch standard deviation** layer that deliberately couples the batch to expose low diversity ([Karras et al., 2018]), and R3GAN removes all normalization. [Kurach et al., 2019] found SN to be the best normalization for discriminators in their study. CHAIN ([Ni et al., 2024]) brought BN back for data-efficient GANs. It shows that BN's centering and scaling steps cause gradient explosion in the discriminator, and replaces centering by a zero-mean regularization and the scaling step by a Lipschitz-constrained root-mean-square normalization. CHAIN reaches state-of-the-art results with limited data. This matches the toolkit's analysis (Section I.6): a normalization works in a critic only if it is per sample and not scale invariant, or if its gain is explicitly bounded.

### Spectral norm penalties versus spectral normalization

[Kurach et al., 2019] and later practice treat SN and gradient penalties as complementary: SN bounds the network everywhere but loosely (the product bound), while a gradient penalty constrains the actual slope where data and samples lie. Combining them is common. SNR ([Yoshida and Miyato, 2017]) sits between the two: it bounds the same quantity as SN, but as a soft loss term that leaves the forward pass unchanged.

| | Spectral normalization | Spectral norm regularization | Gradient penalty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Form | reparametrization $W / \sigma(W)$ | loss $\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_l \sigma_l^2$ (or hinge) | loss on $\|\nabla_x D\|$ |
| Constraint | hard per layer, global through the product bound | soft per layer | soft, local at sampled points |
| Tightness | loose (product bound) | loose | tight where evaluated |
| Cost | power-iteration step per layer | same | about 6F (exact), 1F to 7F (finite differences) |
| Double backward | no | no | yes (exact) |
| Cancelled by scale-invariant norm | yes | yes (acts as weight decay) | no |

## II.7 How strongly to constrain: the case against over-regularization

Several results warn that a strong Lipschitz constraint can hurt:

- With norm-constrained weights and ReLU, the critic is too weak to represent the optimal Wasserstein critic ([Anil et al., 2019]).
- SN shrinks activations with depth ([Lin et al., 2021]), and the per-layer product bound is far looser than the true constant, so a network whose layers all have $\sigma = 1$ is far less than 1-Lipschitz. The toolkit's empirical Lipschitz example found estimates at least ten times below its bound (`examples/nets/efficientnet_empirical_lipschitz.py`).
- A gradient penalty that is too strong turns $D$ into a nearly constant function and slows learning. On the Dirac-GAN (Section II.2), $\gamma > 2 f'(0)$ makes both eigenvalues real: the dynamics become overdamped, and the slow eigenvalue $\lambda \approx -f'(0)^2 / \gamma$ shrinks as $\gamma$ grows. R3GAN tunes $\gamma$ per dataset.

The usual stance is to constrain the critic at the points that matter (zero-centered penalties at data and samples) and to bound it loosely everywhere else (SN), not to make it exactly 1-Lipschitz.

## II.8 Noise, augmentation, and consistency regularization

### Instance noise

**Instance noise** ([Sønderby et al., 2017]; [Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017]) adds Gaussian noise to both real and generated samples before the discriminator, $\tilde P = P * \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$, so the supports overlap and the divergence is informative. The noise level is annealed toward zero during training. By the noise–penalty equivalence (Sections I.4 and II.5), instance noise acts like a zero-centered gradient penalty, and [Mescheder et al., 2018] proved local convergence for both. The difference from the approximated $R_1$ is where the noise enters: instance noise changes the inputs of the loss, while aR1 adds a separate penalty term and leaves the loss on clean inputs.

**Diffusion-GAN** ([Wang et al., 2023]) generalizes instance noise: it diffuses real and generated samples with a forward diffusion process at a random timestep, conditions $D$ on the timestep, and adapts the maximum timestep from the discriminator's overfitting, like ADA's augmentation probability below. Since the noise is applied to both distributions, it does not leak into the generator.

### Data augmentation without leaking

Augmenting only real images teaches the generator to produce augmented images. Three answers appeared in 2020 and 2021:

- **DiffAugment** ([Zhao et al., 2020]) applies the same differentiable augmentations (translation, cutout, color) to real and generated samples, in the discriminator and the generator steps. The generator's gradient flows through the augmentation, which is why it must be differentiable.
- **ADA** ([Karras et al., 2020b]) applies a large pipeline of augmentations with probability $p$, adapted by an overfitting heuristic $r_t = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{sign}(D(x_\text{train}))]$ with target 0.6: $p$ rises when $r_t$ exceeds the target and falls otherwise. It shows that augmentations are non-leaking if they are invertible at the distribution level, which holds for $p < 1$ for most transforms. With 2k images, ADA matches StyleGAN2 trained on an order of magnitude more data.
- **APA** ([Jiang et al., 2021]) adaptively presents generated images as real ones ("pseudo-augmentation"), deceiving the discriminator to counter overfitting.

### Consistency regularization

**CR-GAN** ([Zhang et al., 2020]) penalizes the discriminator's change under augmentation of real images, $\lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{P_r} \|D(x) - D(T(x))\|^2$. **bCR** applies the same to generated images, and **zCR** makes the generator sensitive to latent perturbations while keeping $D$ invariant ([Zhao et al., 2021]). Consistency terms do not let augmentations leak, because $D$ is trained to be invariant to them rather than to see only augmented data. The structure of CR is the same as the approximated $R_1$ of Section II.5: a squared difference of $D$ at a point and at a perturbed copy. The difference is the perturbation, a semantic augmentation in CR and isotropic Gaussian noise in aR1. CR with small additive noise as $T$ is aR1.

### Regularizers against discriminator overfitting

- **LeCam regularization** ([Tseng et al., 2021]) penalizes the distance of the discriminator's outputs from moving averages of its outputs on the other distribution,

  $$\mathcal{R}_\text{LC} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_r} \|D(x) - \alpha_F\|^2 + \mathbb{E}_{z} \|D(G(z)) - \alpha_R\|^2,$$

  where $\alpha_R$ and $\alpha_F$ are exponential moving averages of $D$ on real and fake data. The authors connect it to the LeCam divergence, which is more robust under limited data. It needs only the discriminator outputs the loss already computed (sketch in Part IV).
- **DigGAN** ([Fang et al., 2022]) penalizes the gap between the discriminator's gradient norms on real and generated data, a relative of R3GAN's observation that balanced gradient norms indicate a healthy discriminator.
- **NICE** ([Ni and Koniusz, 2023]) injects adaptive multiplicative noise into the discriminator's features, which lowers its Rademacher complexity, and enforces consistency across noise draws, which penalizes first- and second-order gradients of the features.
- **InsGen** ([Yang et al., 2021]) adds instance-discrimination contrastive tasks to the discriminator. Self-supervised rotation prediction ([Chen et al., 2019]) is an earlier auxiliary task with the same aim, to keep the discriminator's features from forgetting.
- **Flooding** ([Yahiro et al., 2023]) floors the discriminator loss so it cannot become too small, applying the flooding regularizer of supervised learning to GANs.

## II.9 Pretrained feature networks as discriminators

A different route to a well-behaved discriminator is to build it on a frozen pretrained network:

- **Projected GAN** ([Sauer et al., 2021]) uses a frozen EfficientNet-Lite as feature network, with random fixed projections across scales and small spectrally normalized discriminator heads. EfficientNet-Lite drops squeeze-and-excitation and replaces SiLU with ReLU6, and the frozen batch normalization runs in eval mode as a fixed affine map, so the trainable part is small and SN-bounded. Projected GANs converge up to 40 times faster than StyleGAN2-ADA.
- **Vision-aided GAN** ([Kumari et al., 2022]) ensembles off-the-shelf models (CLIP, DINO, Swin) as additional discriminators and selects them by linear-probe accuracy.
- **StyleGAN-XL** ([Sauer et al., 2022]) scales Projected GANs to ImageNet at $1024^2$. **GigaGAN** ([Kang et al., 2023]) uses multi-scale discriminators with $R_1$ and matching-aware losses for text-to-image synthesis.
- **ADD and LADD** ([Sauer et al., 2023]; [Sauer et al., 2024]) distill diffusion models with discriminators on DINOv2 features (ADD) or on the generative features of a pretrained latent diffusion model (LADD).

Frozen features regularize by restriction: the discriminator can only use features that generalize, and it has few trainable parameters to overfit. [Kynkäänniemi et al., 2023] caution that ImageNet-pretrained discriminators can game FID, which uses the same kind of features. The toolkit's 2026.010 exploration notes Projected GAN's EfficientNet-Lite design as an example of an EfficientNet critic that sidesteps the batch-normalization problem. For scientific 1D data no comparable pretrained feature network exists, which makes the explicit regularizers of this part more important there.

## II.10 Generator-side regularization

Most regularization in GANs targets the discriminator, but several methods act on the generator:

- **EMA of generator weights** ([Karras et al., 2018]; [Yazıcı et al., 2019]): evaluate $\bar\theta_G$, an exponential moving average of the generator's weights. It damps the oscillations of the game (the orbit of Section II.2 averages to the equilibrium) and improves FID substantially at no training cost. Every modern GAN uses it.
- **Orthogonal regularization** in BigGAN ([Brock et al., 2019]): $\beta \|W^\top W \odot (\mathbf{1} - I)\|_F^2$ with $\beta = 10^{-4}$, which penalizes only the off-diagonal terms. It makes the generator amenable to the truncation trick.
- **Path-length regularization** ([Karras et al., 2020a]): $\mathbb{E}_{w, y} (\|J_w^\top y\|_2 - a)^2$, with $J_w$ the Jacobian of the generator with respect to the intermediate latent $w$, $y$ a random image, and $a$ a running average. It encourages a fixed-size step in $w$ to produce a fixed-magnitude image change, which makes the latent space smoother.
- **Jacobian clamping** ([Odena et al., 2018]): clamps the generator's Jacobian singular values to a range, after the finding that the Jacobian's condition number early in training predicts final quality.
- **Mode seeking** ([Mao et al., 2019]): maximizes $d(G(c, z_1), G(c, z_2)) / d(z_1, z_2)$ to counter mode collapse in conditional GANs.
- **Feature matching and minibatch discrimination** ([Salimans et al., 2016]): the generator matches intermediate discriminator features, and the discriminator sees batch statistics to detect low diversity. The same paper introduced one-sided label smoothing for the discriminator and historical weight averaging.
- **Mixing regularization** ([Karras et al., 2019]): StyleGAN mixes two latents at a random layer so that styles stay localized.
- **Intermediate supervision**: GAT ([Hyun et al., 2025]) supervises the early layers of its transformer generator to prevent them from being underused, and CAT ([Hyun et al., 2026]) adds a generator-side consistency term that aligns the intermediate outputs of a multi-stage one-step generator with its final output, reporting an FID of 1.56 on class-conditional ImageNet-256.

## II.11 Optimization-side regularization of the game

Several methods regularize the training dynamics rather than either network:

- **TTUR** ([Heusel et al., 2017]): different learning rates for $D$ and $G$, with a convergence proof under stochastic approximation assumptions.
- **Multiple critic steps** per generator step ($n_\text{critic}$, five in WGAN-GP), which keep the critic close to its optimum.
- **Consensus optimization** ([Mescheder et al., 2017]): add $\frac{\gamma}{2} \|v(\theta)\|^2$, the squared norm of the game's gradient field, to both players' losses. It damps the rotational component of the dynamics, which on the Dirac-GAN is exactly the imaginary part of the eigenvalues.
- **Extragradient and optimistic methods** ([Gidel et al., 2019]; [Daskalakis et al., 2018]): look-ahead steps that converge on bilinear games where simultaneous gradient descent cycles.
- **Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$ or small $\beta_1$**: momentum amplifies oscillations; StyleGAN2 uses $\beta_1 = 0$ and R3GAN keeps it.
- **Width-aware learning rates** (GAT, Section II.5): the learning rate scales inversely with the channel count, which keeps larger discriminators stable.

## II.12 What large-scale comparisons found, and reference recipes

- [Lucic et al., 2018] ("Are GANs created equal?") found that, with enough hyperparameter search, most GAN losses reach similar FID, and that differences between papers often come from tuning budgets.
- [Kurach et al., 2019] compared losses, normalizations, penalties, and architectures in a large study. They recommend the non-saturating loss with SN as a default, found that a gradient penalty helps in addition, especially in high-data regimes, and found batch normalization in the discriminator harmful.
- [Li et al., 2023] survey regularization and normalization in GANs and conclude that Lipschitz methods, data augmentation, and consistency regularization are the three pillars, with no single method dominating across settings.

## II.14 Practical concerns: cost, compilation, precision, and randomness

**Cost.** A gradient penalty costs a forward pass, a backward pass to get $\nabla_x D$, and a double backward to differentiate it; the toolkit measured about 6F per critic step. Lazy regularization divides this by the interval. Finite-difference penalties cost forward passes only. SN costs almost nothing.

**Compilation and parallelism.** Double backward is unsupported in `torch.compile`'s AOTAutograd, in fully sharded data parallelism, with gradient checkpointing, and with fused attention kernels. This is the motivation of the approximated $R_1$ of APT and GAT and of the toolkit's finite-difference penalties. Both lines of work arrive at the same design: estimate the slope by differences of critic outputs.

**Precision.** Finite differences of small perturbations are sensitive to reduced precision: perturbations of radius $10^{-2}$ fall within a few bfloat16 ulps of the outputs. The toolkit evaluates its regularizers outside autocast by default. Dividing by $\sigma^2$ amplifies rounding errors by the same factor, so a forward-only penalty in bfloat16 needs a larger $\sigma$ or full-precision forwards; APT's larger noise scale for video ($\sigma = 0.1$) may partly reflect this.

**Randomness in the critic.** A penalty that evaluates the critic more than once (finite differences, CT-GAN's two dropout draws) measures mask noise if the critic contains dropout or drop path. The toolkit measured mean difference quotients of about 1 in train mode against 0.07 to 0.16 in eval mode with `Dropout(0.1)`. CT-GAN uses this noise on purpose, as a perturbation; for a slope estimate it is a bug. Modern discriminators contain no dropout.

**Per-sample penalties and batch statistics.** Batch normalization in the critic makes the per-sample gradient ill-defined (Section I.6); minibatch standard deviation layers couple samples on purpose. A penalty evaluated on a batch of perturbed points also sees different batch statistics than the loss's batch.

**Scale of the input.** Gradient norms, perturbation radii, and the noise scale $\sigma$ are absolute quantities in input units. A penalty tuned for images in $[-1, 1]$ does not transfer to data with another scale; standardize the data or rescale $\sigma$, `radius`, and $\gamma$ with it. The $R_1$ heuristic $\gamma \propto N$ (Section II.5) is another reminder that the penalty depends on the input dimension.

## II.15 Discriminators for 1D signals

The toolkit's critics are 1D convolutional networks. The largest body of work on 1D GAN discriminators is in audio synthesis, and its regularization differs from image GANs:

- **WaveGAN** ([Donahue et al., 2019]) adapted DCGAN to raw audio with a WGAN-GP critic and introduced **phase shuffle**: each discriminator layer shifts its activations by a random offset of up to $n$ samples ($n = 2$), with reflection padding. The generator's transposed convolutions produce periodic artifacts at fixed phases, and phase shuffle prevents the discriminator from relying on the phase of these artifacts. It is a data augmentation inside the network, applied to real and generated inputs alike, so it does not leak.
- **MelGAN** ([Kumar et al., 2019]) uses a multi-scale discriminator: three identical discriminators operate on the raw waveform and on two average-pooled versions. All layers of generator and discriminator use **weight normalization**, the loss is the hinge loss, and a feature-matching loss on the discriminator's intermediate features trains the generator.
- **HiFi-GAN** ([Kong et al., 2020]) adds a **multi-period discriminator** that reshapes the 1D signal into a 2D array with period $p \in \{2, 3, 5, 7, 11\}$, so each sub-discriminator sees samples spaced $p$ apart, alongside MelGAN's multi-scale discriminator. It uses the least-squares loss, feature matching, and a mel-spectrogram reconstruction loss, with weight normalization in all sub-discriminators except the first multi-scale one, which uses spectral normalization.
- **UnivNet** ([Jang et al., 2021]) adds a multi-resolution spectrogram discriminator, and **BigVGAN** ([Lee et al., 2023]) combines multi-period and multi-resolution discriminators with periodic (Snake) activations in the generator to scale vocoders to large models.

Three lessons transfer to time series:

1. **Structure is a regularizer.** Multi-scale, multi-period, and spectrogram discriminators are ensembles of small discriminators, each restricted to one view of the signal. They regularize by restriction, as frozen features do for images.
2. **Reconstruction and feature-matching losses carry much of the training signal.** Conditional vocoders have a paired target, which makes them far more stable than unconditional GANs. An unconditional time-series GAN does not have this, so the discriminator penalties of this part matter more for it.
3. **Weight normalization, not spectral normalization, is the default.** Weight normalization fixes the norm of each filter to a learned scale. It does not bound the Lipschitz constant, because the scale is learnable, but it decouples the scale from the direction and stabilizes training.

Augmentations for time-series discriminators follow the same leak-free rules as Section II.8. Shifts, crops, and time masking are natural, and phase shuffle is a cheap in-network variant. Amplitude scaling is suitable only if amplitude carries no meaning, because a critic must see amplitude errors of the generator (`docs/features/2026.010__efficientnet_arch_mod__1-explore.md`, Section 1.3).

# Part III: Cross-Cutting Themes

## III.1 Lipschitz control, three ways

GAN discriminators constrain the Lipschitz constant in three ways with different guarantees:

| Mechanism | Examples | Guarantee | Cost | Where it constrains |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Architecture | SN, orthogonal and 1-Lipschitz layers, GroupSort, CenterNorm | hard, via the product bound (loose) | low | everywhere |
| Loss on weights | SNR, orthogonality, weight decay | soft | negligible | everywhere, through weights |
| Loss on the function | GP, $R_1$/$R_2$, finite differences, CR, aR1 | soft | high (GP) to moderate (finite differences) | at sampled points only |

Practice combines them: a weak global bound from the architecture (SN, or no normalization at all) and a precise local penalty at the points that matter (data and samples).

## III.2 Where to evaluate a penalty

The choice of evaluation points is as important as the form of the penalty, and the literature has tried every option:

| Points | Methods | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| real data | $R_1$, DRAGAN, CR, aR1, CT-GAN | the discriminator should be flat on the data at equilibrium |
| generated samples | $R_2$, bCR, aR2 | balance; R3GAN shows $R_1$ alone fails for RpGAN |
| interpolates | WGAN-GP, WGAN-LP, zero-centered GP of Thanh-Tung | optimal critic has unit slope along transport segments |
| adversarial directions | ALP | penalize the largest violation |
| everywhere (implicitly) | SN, SNR | product bound |

## III.3 Noise is a gradient penalty

Instance noise, Roth's regularizer, consistency regularization with additive noise, and approximated $R_1$ are all expansions of the same identity: averaging a squared difference over small Gaussian perturbations adds, to second order, a penalty on the squared input gradient ([Bishop, 1995]; derivation in Section II.5). Noise is therefore a cheap way to get a gradient penalty without double backward, at the cost of variance and of a curvature bias at larger noise scales. This explains why the finite-difference penalties of the toolkit, APT, and GAT work, and it suggests that the choice between noise and an explicit penalty is mostly one of cost and variance. The companion survey shows the same identity at work in normalizing flows (Gaussian dequantization and noise augmentation) and EBMs (denoising score matching).

## III.4 Normalization and weight constraints interact

Scale-invariant normalization cancels weight-based constraints (SN, SNR, weight decay) on the layers before it, up to the next non-homogeneous layer. Weight decay then becomes an effective-learning-rate control (Section I.2), and SN changes only the initialization and the optimization dynamics (the toolkit's 2026.009 usage guide). Discriminators avoid the problem by using no normalization (StyleGAN2, R3GAN), SN only (SNGAN, BigGAN), or Lipschitz-bounded normalizations (CHAIN, CenterNorm).

## III.5 Adaptive and scheduled regularization

Regularization strength is rarely constant in the best methods: ADA adapts the augmentation probability to an overfitting heuristic, Diffusion-GAN adapts its noise level the same way, instance noise is annealed, EfficientNetV2 ramps regularization with image size, and LeCam regularization uses moving averages of the discriminator's outputs. Lazy regularization is a schedule of a different kind, applying an expensive penalty less often. The heuristic of ADA ($\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{sign}(D(x_\text{train}))]$, or the gap between training and validation outputs) is a reusable signal for any adaptive scheme.

## III.6 The discriminator as an energy

The discriminator of a GAN defines an energy. [Zhao et al., 2017] trained the discriminator as an energy function (EBGAN), and [Che et al., 2020] showed that a trained GAN defines an energy-based model in latent space, $p(z) \propto p_Z(z) \exp(D(G(z)))$, which can be sampled with Langevin dynamics to improve samples. A smooth, regularized discriminator is therefore also a better energy function, and the regularizers of EBMs (energy-magnitude penalties, SN, $R_1$) mirror those of GANs. The companion survey covers EBMs.

## III.7 Open problems

- **Theory of zero-centered penalties far from equilibrium.** The convergence proofs are local, and R3GAN shows that the practical choice ($R_1$ alone or $R_1 + R_2$) depends on the loss. A global theory is missing.
- **Tight, cheap Lipschitz bounds for modern blocks.** Squeeze-and-excitation gates, attention, and normalizations have no useful global bound, so blocks built from them escape the product bound. Lipschitz-aware replacements exist (CenterNorm, LipsFormer, GroupSort) but are rarely evaluated in GAN critics.
- **Regularizing transformer discriminators at scale.** Approximated $R_1$ works in practice, but its noise scale, its variance in high dimension, and its curvature bias are not understood.
- **Data-efficient training beyond images.** Augmentation pipelines for GANs are designed for images. Time series, physical fields, and scientific data need augmentations that are non-leaking and respect the data's symmetries and units.

| Weight decay, decoupled weight decay ([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019]) | optimizer configs in `dlk/opt/optimizer.py` (`weight_decay`, `decoupled_weight_decay`, AdamW) | Acts as effective-learning-rate control on scale-invariant layers |

The literature's reference points relate to the toolkit's penalty convention as follows. Every `dlk` penalty carries a factor $\frac{1}{2}$, so a weight $\lambda$ from the literature corresponds to `reg_param = 2 * lambda`, and $R_1$'s $\gamma$ corresponds to `reg_param = gamma` for the zero-centered form (which also has the $\frac{1}{2}$).

## IV.2 Gaps, ranked

The ranking weighs the evidence in the literature, the fit with the toolkit's focus on compiled 1D convolutional critics, and the implementation effort.

1. **Zero-centered $R_1$ and $R_2$ at data and generated points.** The strongest current evidence (Mescheder et al., StyleGAN2, R3GAN, GigaGAN) is for zero-centered penalties at the data, and `gradient_penalty` evaluates only at interpolates. A small extension would add an evaluation-point option (`"interpolate"`, `"data"`, `"gen"`) and document the zero-centered settings (`lip=0`, identity nonlinearity). It needs `eager=True` with a compiled critic, like the existing gradient penalty.
2. **Approximated $R_1$ and $R_2$ ([Lin et al., 2025]; [Hyun et al., 2025]).** A forward-only $R_1$: $\frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E} \|D(x + \sigma \xi) - D(x)\|^2 / \sigma^2$ on real data, and the same on generated data for $R_2$. It is a near relative of `gradient_penalty_fd_random`, which perturbs interpolates on a sphere of fixed radius and penalizes the quotient against a target: evaluate at data points, draw Gaussian perturbations, and use target 0. This brings the strongest family of penalties to compiled critics, the toolkit's main use case. Sketch in IV.3.
3. **Lazy regularization.** Call `d_reg_fn` every $k$ discriminator steps and multiply it by $k$ ([Karras et al., 2020a]). A training-loop option; it divides the 6F cost of the gradient penalty by $k$.
4. **Output-based regularizers.** LeCam regularization ([Tseng et al., 2021]) and the drift penalty ([Karras et al., 2018]) need only `d_outputs_gen` and `d_outputs_data`, which `d_reg_fn` already receives. LeCam needs moving averages as state, as `SpectralNormPenalty` keeps its power-iteration state. Both are a few lines and cost nothing. Sketch in IV.3.
5. **EMA of generator weights** ([Yazıcı et al., 2019]). Universal in modern GANs; `torch.optim.swa_utils.AveragedModel` provides it. It belongs in `dlk/opt/train_gan.py` as an option and in the checkpoint.
6. **Discriminator augmentation for 1D signals.** DiffAugment or ADA with transforms for time series (shifts, crops, time masking, additive noise) and an adaptive probability, or an in-network phase shuffle ([Donahue et al., 2019]). Consistency regularization (bCR) is the leak-free alternative.
7. **Instance noise or Diffusion-GAN-style noise.** Cheap and compile-friendly, and it can replace a gradient penalty (Section III.3); an annealed or adaptive noise level is needed.
8. **Relativistic pairing loss.** Needed for an R3GAN- or GAT-style recipe; a new file next to `dlk/loss/hinge_gan.py`.
9. **Lipschitz-aware components for critics.** GroupSort activations ([Anil et al., 2019]) for a tighter Wasserstein estimate, CenterNorm ([Qi et al., 2023]) as a non-scale-invariant per-sample normalization, and an SN coefficient $c$ in `set_spectral_norm`, which invertible residual flows also need (companion survey).
10. **An exact or tighter operator norm for full convolutions.** C2 covers depthwise convolutions; Gram iteration ([Delattre et al., 2024]) or operator power iteration ([Farnia et al., 2019]) would remove the $\sqrt{k}$ factor of C0 on the stem, fused, and dense convolutions.

## IV.3 Code sketches

The sketches show how gaps 2 and 4 fit the existing `d_reg_fn` hook. They are not part of `dlk`, untested, and omit logging (any `dlog` key must be registered in `MONITOR_BASENAMES`).

An approximated $R_1$ (or $R_2$, with `x_gen` in place of `x_data`), normalized by $\sigma^2$ so that `reg_param` plays the role of $\gamma$:

```python
import torch

def make_approximated_r1(sigma: float = 0.01):
    def d_reg_fn(d_net, x_gen, x_data, y_data, *, d_outputs_gen=None, d_outputs_data=None, dlog=None):
        def critic(x):
            return d_net(x, y_data) if y_data is not None else d_net(x)

        # evaluate both points afresh, in full precision, for an accurate difference
        x_noisy = x_data + sigma * torch.randn_like(x_data)
        diff = critic(x_noisy) - critic(x_data)
        return 0.5 * diff.pow(2).flatten(1).sum(dim=1).mean() / sigma**2

    return d_reg_fn
```

Reusing `d_outputs_data` in place of `critic(x_data)` saves one forward pass, but under bfloat16 autocast its rounding error is amplified by $1 / \sigma^2$; this is the same trade-off as the toolkit's endpoint variant.

LeCam regularization, with moving averages as state:

```python
import torch

class LeCamRegularizer:
    def __init__(self, decay: float = 0.99):
        self.decay = decay
        self.ema_data = torch.zeros(())
        self.ema_gen = torch.zeros(())

    def __call__(self, d_net, x_gen, x_data, y_data, *, d_outputs_gen=None, d_outputs_data=None, dlog=None):
        assert d_outputs_gen is not None and d_outputs_data is not None
        with torch.no_grad():
            self.ema_data = self.decay * self.ema_data.to(d_outputs_data) + (1 - self.decay) * d_outputs_data.mean()
            self.ema_gen = self.decay * self.ema_gen.to(d_outputs_gen) + (1 - self.decay) * d_outputs_gen.mean()
        # pull real outputs toward the fake average and fake outputs toward the real average
        return (d_outputs_data - self.ema_gen).pow(2).mean() + (d_outputs_gen - self.ema_data).pow(2).mean()
```

Under DDP, each rank keeps its own averages unless they are all-reduced, the same caveat as the per-rank power-iteration state of `SpectralNormPenalty`.

## IV.4 A configuration guide for `dlk` critics

The literature and the toolkit's measurements suggest the following starting points for a 1D convolutional critic. They are hypotheses to test, not results.

| Goal | Network | Regularizer | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wasserstein critic, eager | `NET_EXACT_SN_FLOORED_PRE_GN`, no dropout | `gradient_penalty` (one-sided, `reg_param = 20`) | the WGAN-LP form of the toolkit's default |
| Wasserstein critic, compiled | same | `gradient_penalty_fd_adversarial`, or `_segment` | retune the weight; finite differences bound the slope from below |
| Hinge or LS critic | `NET_SN_*` preset or `NET_BASELINE` without SN | zero-centered $R_1$ (gap 1) or aR1 (gap 2) | the R3GAN and GAT evidence is for zero-centered penalties |
| Limited data | any of the above | add LeCam (gap 4) and leak-free augmentation (gap 6) | adapt the augmentation strength to an overfitting signal |
| Cheapest global bound | any SN preset | `SpectralNormPenalty(max_norm=1.0)` or SN alone | loose; combine with a local penalty where possible |

In every case: turn off `block_dropout` and the head dropout, keep the regularizer outside autocast, standardize the data so that `radius`, `xi`, and $\sigma$ are meaningful, and evaluate the generator with an EMA of its weights once gap 5 exists.

## IV.5 Consistency with the literature

The toolkit's design choices match the literature on four points:

- Its finite-difference penalties and the approximated $R_1$ of APT and GAT are independent answers to the same problem, double backward under compilation, and both rest on the noise–penalty equivalence.
- Its defaults (one-sided penalty, hinge `SpectralNormPenalty`) follow [Petzka et al., 2018] and [Liu et al., 2019].
- Its finding that batch normalization cancels SN in EfficientNet agrees with [Gulrajani et al., 2017], [Kurach et al., 2019], and CHAIN ([Ni et al., 2024]); its floored pre-norm presets are a Lipschitz-bounded normalization in the spirit of CHAIN and CenterNorm.
- Its rule of no dropout in critics agrees with every modern discriminator design.

The main divergence is that the toolkit's penalties all evaluate at interpolates (WGAN-GP's choice), while the field has moved to zero-centered penalties at data and samples for non-Wasserstein losses. Gaps 1 and 2 close it.
