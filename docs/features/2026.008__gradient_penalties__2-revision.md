# Revision: Gradient Penalties, Finite-Difference Penalties, and Spectral Norm Regularization

## Context

Plan [2026.008](2026.008__gradient_penalties__1-plan.md) (the 1-plan) was implemented in full, and its addendum records the deviations found during implementation. A review of the result, in commits `3078861` and `ce2db60`, then revised the implementation. This document restates the mathematics of all penalties in their revised form (Parts A to C), summarizes the implementation as it stands at `Base-Commit` (Section 1), and lists how it differs from the 1-plan (Section 2). The 1-plan and its addendum are left unchanged as the historical record.

**Why the revision.** The review found that the penalties did not share one logic.

- The autograd gradient penalty came as two functions with different shapes: `gradient_penalty_lip` penalized the squared norm one-sided with a margin, and `gradient_penalty_opt` penalized the norm two-sided with a fixed target of 1 and a hidden square-root guard.
- The finite-difference penalties penalized the quotient itself, $\mathrm{relu}(q - k)^2$ and $(q - k)^2$, which is a different shape from the one-sided autograd penalty.
- The adversarial and random variants were one-sided only, and the endpoint variant had a signature of its own without `d_net`.
- Regularizers received the critic outputs only if they declared them (opt-in keywords found by signature inspection), and the logging keys differed per penalty.

**Settled design decisions.**

- One penalty form for every slope estimate, autograd or finite difference, with the same four arguments `lip`, `eps`, `one_sided`, and `one_sided_nonlinearity` (A.2 and B.0).
- The default one-sided nonlinearity is a softplus with sharpness $\beta = 10$, a smooth approximation of relu. A `relu` can be passed to get a penalty that is exactly zero below the target.
- `eps` defaults to `1e-6` in every penalty. It must be $\ge 0$, and $> 0$ for the two-sided penalty; a violation raises `ValueError`.
- All five penalties share the signature `(d_net, x_gen, x_data, y_data, ...)` of `DiscriminatorRegularizerFn`, take every further argument as keyword-only, and accept the keywords `d_outputs_gen` and `d_outputs_data`.
- Every penalty, including spectral norm regularization, carries a factor $\frac{1}{2}$ and no weight of its own; the consumer's closure applies the regularization weight. `SpectralNormRegularizer` is renamed to `SpectralNormPenalty` and loses its `penalty_weight`.
- The hinge form of `SpectralNormPenalty` takes a nonlinearity `max_norm_nonlinearity`, with the same sharp-softplus default as the one-sided gradient penalties.
- The penalties move from `dlk/loss/wasserstein_gan.py` to a new module `dlk/loss/gradient_penalty.py`, and `dlk/loss/spectral_norm.py` is renamed to `dlk/loss/spectral_penalty.py`.
- The training loop always passes `d_outputs_gen` and `d_outputs_data`, and every regularizer must accept them.

**Core principle.** Every penalty is a function of one per-sample squared slope estimate $s^2$, and the choice of penalty only decides how $s^2$ is estimated. The autograd penalty uses $s^2 = \|g\|^2$, the squared gradient norm; the finite-difference penalties use $s^2 = q^2$, the squared difference quotient. Consequences:

- The penalty shapes, the arguments, their defaults, the validation, and the logging are the same for all five penalties.
- No square root is taken in the one-sided penalty, so it has a finite derivative at a zero slope. The two-sided penalty needs the square root and gets its guard from `eps`.
- The former functions are special cases: the old behavior is reproducible through the arguments (Section 2).

## Terminology

- **Critic**: the network $D$ that scores samples; the code calls it `d_net`. A conditional critic also takes a conditioning input $y$.
- **Lipschitz constant**: the smallest $L$ with $|D(x) - D(x')| \le L \|x - x'\|$ for all $x, x'$. For a differentiable $D$ it equals $\sup_x \|\nabla_x D(x)\|$.
- **Slope estimate** $s$: a per-sample estimate of the critic's local Lipschitz constant, either the gradient norm $\|g\|$ (autograd) or the difference quotient $q$ (finite difference).
- **Difference quotient** $q(x_a, x_b)$: $|D(x_a) - D(x_b)| / \|x_a - x_b\|$, the average slope of $D$ between two points.
- **Interpolate** $\hat{x}$: a random point on the segment between a real sample and a generated sample.
- **Shared penalty form**: the one- and two-sided penalties of A.2 and B.0, applied to $s^2$.
- **One-sided / two-sided penalty**: one-sided penalizes only slopes above the target $k$; two-sided penalizes any deviation from $k$.
- **Sharp softplus**: $\phi(z) = \mathrm{softplus}(\beta z) / \beta = \log(1 + e^{\beta z}) / \beta$ with $\beta = 10$, the default one-sided nonlinearity and the default nonlinearity of the spectral hinge form. It equals $\ln 2 / 10 \approx 0.069$ at $z = 0$, decays like $e^{10 z} / 10$ for $z < 0$, and approaches $z$ for $z > 0$.
- **Margin** and **guard**: the two roles of `eps` (symbol $\varepsilon$). In the one-sided penalty it is a margin that tightens the threshold; in the two-sided penalty it is a guard that keeps the square root differentiable at a zero slope.
- **Double backward**: differentiating a gradient, needed when a loss depends on $\nabla_x D$ and is differentiated with respect to the parameters $\theta$.
- **Spectral norm** $\sigma(W)$: the largest singular value of a matrix $W$, equal to its operator norm $\max_{h \neq 0} \|Wh\| / \|h\|$.
- **Power iteration**: the iterative method that estimates the largest singular value and its singular vectors $u, v$ using only matrix-vector products.

## Part A: Autograd gradient penalty

### A.1 Wasserstein GAN and the Lipschitz constraint

The Wasserstein-1 distance between the data distribution $P_r$ and the generator distribution $P_g$ has the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual form (Villani 2009; Arjovsky et al. 2017)

$$W_1(P_r, P_g) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1} \; E_{x \sim P_r}[f(x)] - E_{x \sim P_g}[f(x)].$$

The critic $D_\theta$ approximates the maximizer $f$, and the losses for the critic and generator steps are

$$\ell_D = -E[D(x_\mathrm{data})] + E[D(x_\mathrm{gen})], \qquad \ell_G = -E[D(x_\mathrm{gen})].$$

Any $K$-Lipschitz critic yields $K \cdot W_1$, so a penalty only has to keep the Lipschitz constant bounded, and the exact value of $K$ does not matter. For the optimal critic $f^*$ and an optimal coupling $\pi^*$ of $P_r$ and $P_g$, $f^*(x) - f^*(y) = \|x - y\|$ holds $\pi^*$-almost surely, so $f^*$ has gradient norm 1 on the segments between coupled points (Gulrajani et al. 2017, Proposition 1). This motivates the two-sided target $k = 1$ and the segment-based sampling of the penalties below.

**Implementation.** `wasserstein_loss_fn` in `dlk/loss/wasserstein_gan.py`, which now holds only the loss.

### A.2 Autograd gradient penalty

**Mathematics.** With $x_\mathrm{data} \sim P_r$, $x_\mathrm{gen} \sim P_g$, and $t \sim U(0, 1)$ drawn per sample,

$$\hat{x} = t \, x_\mathrm{data} + (1 - t) \, x_\mathrm{gen}, \qquad g = \nabla_{(x, y)} D(\hat{x}, y), \qquad s^2 = \|g\|^2 = \|\nabla_x D\|^2 + \|\nabla_y D\|^2,$$

where the $y$ term is present only for a conditional critic. With the target Lipschitz constant $k$ (argument `lip`), the small constant $\varepsilon \ge 0$ (argument `eps`), and the nonlinearity $\phi$ (argument `one_sided_nonlinearity`), the shared penalty form is

$$\mathcal{R}_\mathrm{one} = \frac{1}{2} E[\phi(s^2 + \varepsilon - k^2)] \quad (\texttt{one\_sided=True}), \qquad \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{two} = \frac{1}{2} E[(\sqrt{s^2 + \varepsilon} - k)^2] \quad (\texttt{one\_sided=False}).$$

The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ matches spectral norm regularization (C.2); a weight $\lambda$ from the literature corresponds to a regularization weight $2 \lambda$ here.

- **One-sided.** This follows WGAN-LP (Petzka et al. 2018), applied to the squared norm. WGAN-LP penalizes $E[\mathrm{relu}(\|g\| - k)^2]$; with $\phi = \mathrm{relu}$, the form above vanishes on the same set of points, $\|g\|^2 \le k^2 - \varepsilon$, with a different shape above it. Here $\varepsilon$ is a margin that tightens the threshold. With the default sharp softplus the penalty stays slightly positive below the threshold: at $\|g\| = 0.35$ and $k = 1$ it is about $7.7 \cdot 10^{-6}$ per sample.
- **Two-sided.** For $k = 1$ this is WGAN-GP (Gulrajani et al. 2017), up to the factor $\frac{1}{2}$, which pushes the gradient norm toward the value of the optimal critic (A.1). Here $\varepsilon > 0$ is a guard: the derivative of $\sqrt{s^2}$ is infinite at $s = 0$, and $\sqrt{s^2 + \varepsilon}$ keeps it finite.

**Why double backward.** The parameter gradient of the penalty is

$$\partial_\theta \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{one} = \frac{1}{2} E\big[\phi'(s^2 + \varepsilon - k^2) \, \partial_\theta s^2\big], \qquad \partial_\theta \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{two} = \frac{1}{2} E\Big[\frac{\sqrt{s^2 + \varepsilon} - k}{\sqrt{s^2 + \varepsilon}} \, \partial_\theta s^2\Big],$$

$$\partial_\theta s^2 = \partial_\theta \|\nabla_x D_\theta(\hat{x})\|^2 = 2 \, (\nabla_x D_\theta)^\top \, \partial_\theta \nabla_x D_\theta(\hat{x}),$$

which contains the mixed second derivative $\partial_\theta \nabla_x D_\theta$ (plus the analogous $y$ term). Autograd therefore records the input gradient with `create_graph=True` and differentiates it again in the training step's backward pass. `torch.compile` does not support this double backward, so a compiled critic needs the `eager` keyword, which runs the penalty's critic evaluation uncompiled.

**Implementation.** `gradient_penalty` in `dlk/loss/gradient_penalty.py`, with `_gradient_norm_sq` for $s^2$. Compile workaround and its tests: 1-plan A.2.

## Part B: Finite-difference gradient penalties

### B.0 Shared formulation

For a pair $(x_a, x_b)$ with a shared conditioning input $y$, the squared difference quotient is

$$q^2 = \frac{\|D(x_a, y) - D(x_b, y)\|^2}{\max(\|x_a - x_b\|^2, \delta^2)},$$

with a small $\delta$ (argument `min_dist`) that guards against division by zero; equivalently, $q = \|D(x_a) - D(x_b)\| / \max(\|x_a - x_b\|, \delta)$. The penalties are the shared form of A.2 with $s^2 = q^2$:

$$\mathcal{R}_\mathrm{one} = \frac{1}{2} E[\phi(q^2 + \varepsilon - k^2)] \quad (\texttt{one\_sided=True}), \qquad \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{two} = \frac{1}{2} E[(\sqrt{q^2 + \varepsilon} - k)^2] \quad (\texttt{one\_sided=False}).$$

**Lower bound.** By the mean value theorem, $q(x_a, x_b) \le \sup_{z \in [x_a, x_b]} \|\nabla_x D(z)\|$. A difference quotient is therefore a lower bound on the local Lipschitz constant, and a finite-difference penalty enforces the constraint more loosely than the gradient penalty at the same points. As $x_b \to x_a$ along a unit direction $u$, $q \to |\nabla_x D(x_a)^\top u|$, the directional derivative. The choice of direction decides how close $q$ comes to $\|\nabla_x D\|$, and the four variants below differ exactly in that choice. Because pairs share $y$, the penalties constrain the slope in $x$ only, whereas the autograd penalty includes $\nabla_y D$.

**No double backward.** $q^2$ is a function of two critic evaluations, so its parameter gradient $\partial_\theta q^2 = 2 \, (D(x_a) - D(x_b))^\top \partial_\theta (D(x_a) - D(x_b)) / \max(\|x_a - x_b\|^2, \delta^2)$ needs only the first-order backward pass of the training step, and a compiled critic works without the `eager` keyword. Computing $q^2$ from sums of squares also avoids the derivative of a norm at a zero difference.

**Example: linear critic.** For $D(x) = w^\top x$ and a pair whose difference is parallel to $w$, $q^2 = \|w\|^2$ and $\nabla_w q^2 = 2 w$. The one-sided penalty with $\phi = \mathrm{relu}$ then equals $\frac{1}{2} (\|w\|^2 - k^2)$ with gradient $w$ above the target, and the two-sided penalty equals $\frac{1}{2} (s - k)^2$ with gradient $(s - k) \, w / s$, where $s = \sqrt{\|w\|^2 + \varepsilon}$.

**Implementation.** `_gradient_fd_norm_sq` and `_gradient_fd_penalty` in `dlk/loss/gradient_penalty.py`, shared by the four variants.

### B.1 Segment pairs

**Mathematics.** Two points on each real-to-generated segment, with $t_a, t_b \sim U(0,1)$ drawn independently per sample,

$$x_a = t_a x_\mathrm{data} + (1 - t_a) x_\mathrm{gen}, \qquad x_b = t_b x_\mathrm{data} + (1 - t_b) x_\mathrm{gen}, \qquad q = \frac{|D(x_a) - D(x_b)|}{\max(|t_a - t_b| \, \|x_\mathrm{data} - x_\mathrm{gen}\|, \delta)}.$$

$q$ is the average slope of $D$ between $x_a$ and $x_b$ in the direction $u = (x_\mathrm{data} - x_\mathrm{gen}) / \|x_\mathrm{data} - x_\mathrm{gen}\|$, the direction in which the optimal critic has slope 1 on coupled pairs (A.1). Real and generated samples in a batch are paired at random, not by an optimal coupling, so the two-sided form is as heuristic here as the autograd two-sided penalty. One- and two-sided.

**Literature.** Gulrajani et al. 2017; Petzka et al. 2018; Wei et al. 2018.

**Implementation.** `gradient_penalty_fd_segment`; two critic evaluations.

### B.2 Endpoint pairs

**Mathematics.** The quotient at the real and generated endpoints themselves,

$$q_i = \frac{|D(x_{\mathrm{data},i}) - D(x_{\mathrm{gen},i})|}{\max(\|x_{\mathrm{data},i} - x_{\mathrm{gen},i}\|, \delta)}.$$

$q_i$ is the average slope over the whole segment, the loosest of the segment-based bounds. Its numerator is the difference of the critic outputs that the Wasserstein loss already computed, so the penalty needs no extra critic evaluation. One- and two-sided.

**Literature.** Villani 2009 and Gulrajani et al. 2017 (the optimal critic satisfies $q = 1$ on coupled pairs); Wei et al. 2018.

**Implementation.** `gradient_penalty_fd_endpoint`; takes the loss's outputs through the keywords `d_outputs_gen` and `d_outputs_data`, ignores `d_net` and `y_data`, and raises `ValueError` without the outputs.

### B.3 Adversarial direction

**Mathematics.** Adversarial Lipschitz Regularization (Terjék 2020) searches, at an interpolate $\hat{x}$ as in A.2, for the perturbation that most violates the constraint,

$$r^* = \arg\max_{\|r\| \le \rho} |D(\hat{x}) - D(\hat{x} + r)|,$$

approximated by one power-iteration step as in virtual adversarial training (Miyato et al. 2018, VAT). Starting from a random direction $d \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ of size $\xi$ (argument `xi`),

$$r_0 = \xi \, \frac{d}{\|d\|}, \qquad g = \nabla_r \, |D(\hat{x}) - D(\hat{x} + r)| \big|_{r = r_0}, \qquad r_\mathrm{adv} = \rho \, \frac{g}{\|g\|},$$

with $\rho$ (argument `radius`), and the pair is $(\hat{x}, \hat{x} + r_\mathrm{adv})$. For small $\xi$, $g$ is parallel to $\nabla_x D(\hat{x})$, so $q \approx \|\nabla_x D(\hat{x})\|$, and for a linear critic this is exact after one step. The adversarial variant is therefore the only finite-difference variant that approximates the gradient norm itself, which also makes the two-sided form with $k = 1$ as meaningful here as for the autograd penalty. Where the critic is flat at $\hat{x}$ ($g = 0$), the random direction $d$ is used. $g$ is a first-order input gradient, and $r_\mathrm{adv}$ is treated as a constant, so the penalty is an ordinary function of two critic evaluations. One-sided by default, two-sided available.

**Literature.** Terjék 2020; Miyato et al. 2018 (VAT).

**Implementation.** `gradient_penalty_fd_adversarial`; three critic evaluations and one input backward pass; the search runs on the DDP-unwrapped critic (1-plan B.3 and addendum step 9).

### B.4 Random perturbations (baseline)

**Mathematics.** With $u$ uniform on the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and radius $\rho$, the pair is $(\hat{x}, \hat{x} + \rho u)$. For small $\rho$, $q \approx |\nabla_x D(\hat{x})^\top u|$, and

$$E[(\nabla_x D^\top u)^2] = \frac{\|\nabla_x D\|^2}{d}, \qquad E|\nabla_x D^\top u| = \|\nabla_x D\| \, \frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\sqrt{\pi} \, \Gamma((d+1)/2)}.$$

The penalty sees only about a $1/\sqrt{d}$ fraction of the slope, so in high dimension it barely constrains the critic. The two-sided form is available but has no natural target for the same reason. This variant is a baseline for ablations only.

**Literature.** Kodali et al. 2017 (DRAGAN); Miyato et al. 2018 (VAT, random directions as the starting point of power iteration).

**Implementation.** `gradient_penalty_fd_random`; two critic evaluations.

## Part C: Spectral normalization and spectral norm regularization

Both methods control the Lipschitz constant through the spectral norms of the critic's weights instead of slopes at samples. Spectral normalization (C.1) enforces a hard per-layer bound in the architecture; spectral norm regularization (C.2) adds a soft per-layer penalty to the loss.

### C.1 Spectral normalization

**Mathematics.** Spectral normalization (Miyato et al. 2018) replaces each weight matrix by $\bar{W} = W / \sigma(W)$, so that each linear layer is 1-Lipschitz. The spectral norm is estimated by power iteration on persistent vectors $u, v$,

$$v \leftarrow \frac{W^\top u}{\|W^\top u\|}, \qquad u \leftarrow \frac{W v}{\|W v\|}, \qquad \sigma(W) \approx u^\top W v,$$

with one iteration per training forward pass. Lipschitz constants multiply under composition,

$$\mathrm{Lip}(f_L \circ \dots \circ f_1) \le \prod_{l=1}^{L} \mathrm{Lip}(f_l),$$

so the network is 1-Lipschitz if every layer is. This holds for linear layers with $\bar{W}$, 1-Lipschitz activations such as ReLU, and averaged residual combinations $0.5 \, h_\mathrm{skip} + 0.5 \, h_\mathrm{block}$. The bound fails for layer normalization (its local Lipschitz constant grows without bound on nearly constant inputs), for dot-product self-attention (Kim et al. 2021), and for dropout in training mode. For convolutions, the kernel reshaped to a matrix of shape (out channels, in channels × kernel size) is normalized, and the norm of the convolution operator can be larger (Sedghi et al. 2019). The bound is also loose, which limits the critic's capacity but is valid for WGAN, where any $K$ works (A.1).

**Literature.** Miyato et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2021; Sedghi et al. 2019.

**Implementation.** Unchanged by this revision: `enable_spectral_norm` in `dlk/nets/mlp.py` and `dlk/nets/conv1d.py`, orthogonal initialization in `set_init_parameters` (`dlk/nets/utils.py`); details in 1-plan A.3 and plan 2026.007.

### C.2 Spectral norm regularization

**Mathematics.** For every weighted layer $l$ with weight $W_l$ reshaped to a matrix $\tilde{W}_l$ of shape (out features × everything else), the regularizer is

$$\mathcal{R}_\mathrm{SN} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_l \sigma(\tilde{W}_l)^2 \quad (\texttt{max\_norm=None}), \qquad \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{SN} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_l \phi\big(\sigma(\tilde{W}_l) - k\big)^2 \quad (\texttt{max\_norm}=k),$$

with a nonlinearity $\phi$ (argument `max_norm_nonlinearity`). The first form is spectral norm regularization as published (Yoshida and Miyato 2017). The second is its hinge variant, which acts on layers above $k$. With $\phi = \mathrm{relu}$ the hinge is exact; the default sharp softplus stays slightly positive below $k$, with $\phi(0)^2 / 2 = (\ln 2 / 10)^2 / 2 \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-3}$ per layer at the target. Each $\sigma_l$ is estimated by $n$ power-iteration steps per call on a persistent left singular vector $u_l$,

$$v_l \leftarrow \frac{\tilde{W}_l^\top u_l}{\|\tilde{W}_l^\top u_l\|}, \qquad u_l \leftarrow \frac{\tilde{W}_l v_l}{\|\tilde{W}_l v_l\|}, \qquad \sigma_l = u_l^\top \tilde{W}_l v_l.$$

The iteration runs without gradient tracking, so $u_l, v_l$ enter $\sigma_l$ as constants. If they are the top singular vectors, $\partial \sigma_l / \partial \tilde{W}_l = u_l v_l^\top$, and the parameter gradient is the rank-one matrix

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{SN}}{\partial \tilde{W}_l} = \sigma_l \, u_l v_l^\top \quad \text{or} \quad \phi(\sigma_l - k) \, \phi'(\sigma_l - k) \, u_l v_l^\top,$$

which needs only a first-order backward pass.

**Relation to the Lipschitz constraint.** For a critic built from weighted layers and 1-Lipschitz activations, $\mathrm{Lip}(D) \le \prod_l \sigma_l$ (C.1). The hinge form with $k = 1$ pushes every factor toward at most 1 and is a soft version of spectral normalization that leaves the architecture unchanged. The published form also shrinks layers already below 1, like weight decay on the top singular value. Liu et al. 2019 observe that a collapse of the critic's singular values precedes mode collapse, which favors the hinge form for Lipschitz control, with $\phi = \mathrm{relu}$ to leave layers below $k$ untouched. The limitations of C.1 carry over: layer normalization, self-attention, and dropout break the product bound, and for convolutions $\sigma(\tilde{W}_l)$ is the reshaped norm, not the operator norm.

Spectral norm regularization keeps its own form and does not adopt the shared penalty form: it penalizes weights, not slopes at samples, and `max_norm` is a target for each layer's $\sigma_l$, not for a slope. Hence $\phi$ acts on $\sigma_l - k$ and is squared, while the one-sided gradient penalties apply $\phi$ to $s^2 - k^2$. It shares the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ and the default $\phi$.

**Literature.** Yoshida and Miyato 2017; Miyato et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2019.

**Implementation.** `SpectralNormPenalty` in `dlk/loss/spectral_penalty.py` (formerly `SpectralNormRegularizer` in `dlk/loss/spectral_norm.py`); design as in 1-plan C.2, without its `penalty_weight`. Invalid `max_norm`, `n_power_iterations`, or `n_warmup_iterations` raise `ValueError`. Its `__call__` accepts and ignores `d_outputs_gen` and `d_outputs_data`, and it logs one key, `spectral_norm`, the mean of the layers' $\sigma_l$.

## Part D: Cost comparison

Unchanged; see 1-plan Part D and the measured costs in its addendum (step 10). The revision changes only elementwise operations on per-sample scalars, which cost $O(B)$ against the critic evaluations.

**Reproducing the 1-plan penalties.** Each former penalty is a special case of the revised arguments, times 2 for the factor $\frac{1}{2}$.

- `gradient_penalty_lip(..., lip=k, eps=e)` is `2 * gradient_penalty(..., lip=k, eps=e, one_sided_nonlinearity=F.relu)`.
- `gradient_penalty_opt(...)` is `2 * gradient_penalty(..., lip=1.0, eps=1e-12, one_sided=False)`.
- `SpectralNormRegularizer(penalty_weight=w, max_norm=k)` is `w` times `SpectralNormPenalty(max_norm=k, max_norm_nonlinearity=F.relu)`.
- The former finite-difference one-sided $\mathrm{relu}(q - k)^2$ is the one-sided form with `eps=0.0` and $\phi(z) = \mathrm{relu}(\sqrt{z + k^2} - k)^2$, since $z = q^2 - k^2$; both $k$ are `lip`, so $\phi$ must close over the same value. At $q = 0$ the square root has an infinite derivative, which autograd multiplies by the zero relu into NaN; clamp its argument, for example `torch.sqrt((z + k**2).clamp(min=1e-12))`.
- The former finite-difference two-sided $(q - k)^2$ is the two-sided form in the limit $\varepsilon \to 0$; with the default `eps=1e-6`, $\sqrt{q^2 + \varepsilon}$ differs from $q$ by about $\varepsilon / (2 q)$.

**Numerical consequences.** With default arguments, every penalty changes value. Above the threshold, the one-sided finite-difference penalty grows like $q^2 - k^2 = (q - k)(q + k)$ instead of $(q - k)^2$, which is larger by $2 k (q - k)$ for every $q > k$. The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ halves every gradient penalty, and the default spectral hinge is slightly positive below `max_norm`. Regularization weights tuned against the 1-plan penalties need retuning.

## 5. Risks and edge cases

- The sharp softplus is positive below the threshold and still pulls slopes down slightly, with slope $\phi'(z) = \sigma(10 z)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. At the threshold it contributes $\frac{1}{2} \ln 2 / 10$ per sample, with half the slope of relu above it. Pass `F.relu` for an exact zero below the target.
- The same default in the spectral hinge form still shrinks layers below `max_norm` slightly. Pass `max_norm_nonlinearity=F.relu` to leave them untouched, as Liu et al. 2019 suggest.
- The one-sided margin $\varepsilon$ tightens the threshold to $s^2 \le k^2 - \varepsilon$. At the default `1e-6` the effect is negligible for $k$ of order 1, but it grows in relative terms for small $k$.
- The two-sided penalty with a small $\varepsilon$ still has a large derivative near a zero slope: $\partial_{s^2} \sqrt{s^2 + \varepsilon} = 1 / (2 \sqrt{s^2 + \varepsilon})$, about 500 at $s = 0$ with the default.
- `autocast_d_reg_fn=True` runs the finite-difference penalties in low precision. The adversarial and random variants compare critic outputs a small distance apart, and the 1-plan (Section 11 and addendum step 5) measured their quotient biased low under `bfloat16` autocast. Keep the default for these variants.
- Every regularizer must now accept `d_outputs_gen` and `d_outputs_data`. A consumer closure written against the 1-plan's opt-in contract without these keywords raises `TypeError` in the loop.
- The two-sided form of the random variant has no natural target (B.4); it exists for a uniform interface, not as a recommended penalty.
